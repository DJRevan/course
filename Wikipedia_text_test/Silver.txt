**Silver** is a [chemical element](/wiki/Chemical_element "Chemical element"); it has [symbol](/wiki/Symbol_\(chemistry\) "Symbol \(chemistry\)") **Ag** (from [Latin](/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language") __[argentum](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/argentum#Latin "wikt:argentum")__ 'silver') and [atomic number](/wiki/Atomic_number "Atomic number") 47\. A soft, whitish-gray, lustrous [transition metal](/wiki/Transition_metal "Transition metal"), it exhibits the highest [electrical conductivity](/wiki/Electrical_conductivity "Electrical conductivity"), [thermal conductivity](/wiki/Thermal_conductivity "Thermal conductivity"), and [reflectivity](/wiki/Reflectivity "Reflectivity") of any [metal](/wiki/Metal "Metal").[11] Silver is found in the Earth's crust in the pure, free elemental form ("[native silver](/wiki/Native_silver "Native silver")"), as an [alloy](/wiki/Alloy "Alloy") with [gold](/wiki/Gold "Gold") and other metals, and in minerals such as [argentite](/wiki/Argentite "Argentite") and [chlorargyrite](/wiki/Chlorargyrite "Chlorargyrite"). Most silver is produced as a byproduct of [copper](/wiki/Copper "Copper"), gold, [lead](/wiki/Lead "Lead"), and [zinc](/wiki/Zinc "Zinc") [refining](/wiki/Refining_\(metallurgy\) "Refining \(metallurgy\)"). 

Silver has long been valued as a [precious metal](/wiki/Precious_metal "Precious metal"), commonly sold and marketed beside gold and [platinum](/wiki/Platinum "Platinum"). Silver metal is used in many [bullion coins](/wiki/Bullion_coin "Bullion coin"), sometimes [alongside gold](/wiki/Bimetallism "Bimetallism"): while it is more abundant than gold, it is much less abundant as a [native metal](/wiki/Native_metal "Native metal"). Its purity is typically measured on a [per-mille](/wiki/Per-mille "Per-mille") basis; a 94%-pure alloy is described as "0.940 fine". As one of the seven [metals of antiquity](/wiki/Metals_of_antiquity "Metals of antiquity"), silver has had an enduring role in most human cultures. In terms of [scarcity](/wiki/Scarcity "Scarcity"), silver is the most abundant of the big three precious metals, [platinum](/wiki/Platinum_as_an_investment "Platinum as an investment"), gold, and silver; among these, platinum is the rarest, with around 139 [troy ounces](/wiki/Troy_weight "Troy weight") of silver mined for every one of platinum.[12]

Other than in [currency](/wiki/Currency "Currency") and as an [investment](/wiki/Investment "Investment") medium ([coins](/wiki/Coins "Coins") and [bullion](/wiki/Bullion "Bullion")), silver is used in [solar panels](/wiki/Solar_panels "Solar panels"), [water filtration](/wiki/Water_filtration "Water filtration"), [jewellery](/wiki/Jewellery "Jewellery"), ornaments, high-value tableware and utensils (hence the term "[silverware](/wiki/Silver_\(household\) "Silver \(household\)")"), in [electrical contacts](/wiki/Electrical_contact "Electrical contact") and [conductors](/wiki/Electrical_conductor "Electrical conductor"), in specialised mirrors, window coatings, in [catalysis](/wiki/Catalysis "Catalysis") of chemical reactions, as a colorant in [stained glass](/wiki/Stained_glass "Stained glass"), and in specialised confectionery. Its compounds are used in [photographic](/wiki/Photographic_film "Photographic film") and [X-ray](/wiki/X-ray "X-ray") film. Dilute solutions of [silver nitrate](/wiki/Silver_nitrate "Silver nitrate") and other silver compounds are used as [disinfectants](/wiki/Disinfectant "Disinfectant") and microbiocides ([oligodynamic effect](/wiki/Oligodynamic_effect "Oligodynamic effect")), added to [bandages](/wiki/Bandage "Bandage"), wound-dressings, [catheters](/wiki/Catheter "Catheter"), and other [medical instruments](/wiki/Medical_instrument "Medical instrument"). 

## Characteristics

[](/wiki/File:1000oz.silver.bullion.bar.top.jpg)Silver bullion bar, 1000 ounces [](/wiki/File:Ag_atomic_wire.jpg)Silver is extremely ductile and, like gold, can be drawn into a wire one atom wide.[13]

Silver is similar in its physical and chemical properties to its two vertical neighbours in [group 11](/wiki/Group_11_element "Group 11 element") of the [periodic table](/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table"): [copper](/wiki/Copper "Copper"), and [gold](/wiki/Gold "Gold"). Its 47 electrons are arranged in the [configuration](/wiki/Electron_configuration "Electron configuration") [Kr]4d105s1, similarly to copper ([Ar]3d104s1) and gold ([Xe]4f145d106s1); group 11 is one of the few groups in the [d-block](/wiki/D-block "D-block") which has a completely consistent set of electron configurations.[14] This distinctive electron configuration, with a single electron in the highest occupied s [subshell](/wiki/Electron_shell "Electron shell") over a filled d subshell, accounts for many of the singular properties of metallic silver.[15]

Silver is a relatively soft and extremely [ductile](/wiki/Ductility "Ductility") and [malleable](/wiki/Malleability "Malleability") [transition metal](/wiki/Transition_metal "Transition metal"), though it is slightly less malleable than gold. Silver crystallises in a [face-centred cubic](/wiki/Face-centred_cubic "Face-centred cubic") lattice with bulk coordination number 12, where only the single 5s electron is delocalised, similarly to copper and gold.[16] Unlike metals with incomplete d-shells, metallic bonds in silver are lacking a [covalent](/wiki/Covalent_bond "Covalent bond") character and are relatively weak. This observation explains the low [hardness](/wiki/Hardness "Hardness") and high ductility of [single crystals](/wiki/Monocrystalline "Monocrystalline") of silver.[17]

Silver has a brilliant, white, metallic lustre that can take a high [polish](/wiki/Polishing "Polishing"),[18] and which is so characteristic that the name of the metal itself has become a [colour name](/wiki/Silver_\(color\) "Silver \(color\)").[15] Protected silver has greater optical [reflectivity](/wiki/Reflectivity "Reflectivity") than [aluminium](/wiki/Aluminium "Aluminium") at all wavelengths longer than ~450 nm.[19] At wavelengths shorter than 450 nm, silver's reflectivity is inferior to that of aluminium and drops to zero near 310 nm.[20]

Very high electrical and thermal conductivity are common to the elements in group 11, because their single s electron is free and does not interact with the filled d subshell, as such interactions (which occur in the preceding transition metals) lower electron mobility.[21] The [thermal conductivity](/wiki/Thermal_conductivity "Thermal conductivity") of silver is among the highest of all materials, although the thermal conductivity of [carbon](/wiki/Carbon "Carbon") (in the [diamond](/wiki/Diamond "Diamond") [allotrope](/wiki/Allotropy "Allotropy")) and [superfluid helium-4](/wiki/Superfluid_helium-4 "Superfluid helium-4") are higher.[14] The [electrical conductivity](/wiki/Electrical_conductivity "Electrical conductivity") of silver is the highest of all metals, greater even than copper. Silver also has the lowest [contact resistance](/wiki/Contact_resistance "Contact resistance") of any metal.[14] Silver is rarely used for its electrical conductivity, due to its high cost, although an exception is in [radio-frequency engineering](/wiki/Radio-frequency_engineering "Radio-frequency engineering"), particularly at [VHF](/wiki/VHF "VHF") and higher frequencies where silver plating improves electrical conductivity because those [currents tend to flow on the surface of conductors](/wiki/Skin_effect "Skin effect") rather than through the interior. During [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") in the US, 13540 tons of silver were used for the [electromagnets](/wiki/Electromagnets "Electromagnets") in [calutrons](/wiki/Calutron "Calutron") for enriching [uranium](/wiki/Uranium "Uranium"), mainly because of the wartime shortage of copper.[22][23][24]

Silver readily forms [alloys](/wiki/Alloy "Alloy") with copper, gold, and [zinc](/wiki/Zinc "Zinc"). Zinc-silver alloys with low zinc concentration may be considered as face-centred cubic solid solutions of zinc in silver, as the structure of the silver is largely unchanged while the electron concentration rises as more zinc is added. Increasing the electron concentration further leads to [body-centred cubic](/wiki/Body-centred_cubic "Body-centred cubic") (electron concentration 1.5), [complex cubic](/wiki/Cubic_crystal_system "Cubic crystal system") (1.615), and [hexagonal close-packed](/wiki/Hexagonal_close-packed "Hexagonal close-packed") phases (1.75).[16]

### Isotopes

Main article: [Isotopes of silver](/wiki/Isotopes_of_silver "Isotopes of silver")

Naturally occurring silver is composed of two stable [isotopes](/wiki/Isotope "Isotope"), 107Ag and 109Ag, with 107Ag being slightly more abundant (51.839% [natural abundance](/wiki/Natural_abundance "Natural abundance")). This almost equal abundance is rare in the periodic table. The [atomic weight](/wiki/Atomic_weight "Atomic weight") is 107.8682(2) [Da](/wiki/Dalton_\(unit\) "Dalton \(unit\)");[25][26] this value is very important because of the importance of silver compounds, particularly halides, in [gravimetric analysis](/wiki/Gravimetric_analysis "Gravimetric analysis").[25] Both isotopes of silver are produced in stars via the [s-process](/wiki/S-process "S-process") (slow neutron capture), as well as in supernovas via the [r-process](/wiki/R-process "R-process") (rapid neutron capture).[27]

Twenty-eight [radioisotopes](/wiki/Radioisotope "Radioisotope") have been characterised, the most stable being 105Ag with a [half-life](/wiki/Half-life "Half-life") of 41.29 days, 111Ag with a half-life of 7.45 days, and 112Ag with a half-life of 3.13 hours. Silver has numerous [nuclear isomers](/wiki/Nuclear_isomer "Nuclear isomer"), the most stable being 108mAg (_t_ 1/2 = 418 years), 110mAg (_t_ 1/2 = 249.79 days) and 106mAg (_t_ 1/2 = 8.28 days). All of the remaining [radioactive](/wiki/Radioactive "Radioactive") isotopes have half-lives of less than an hour, and the majority of these have half-lives of less than three minutes.[28]

Isotopes of silver range in [atomic mass](/wiki/Atomic_mass "Atomic mass") from 92.950 Da (93Ag) to 129.950 Da (130Ag);[29] the primary [decay mode](/wiki/Decay_mode "Decay mode") before the most abundant stable isotope, 107Ag, is [electron capture](/wiki/Electron_capture "Electron capture") and the primary mode after is [beta decay](/wiki/Beta_decay "Beta decay"). The primary [decay products](/wiki/Decay_product "Decay product") before 107Ag are [palladium](/wiki/Palladium "Palladium") (element 46) isotopes, and the primary products after are [cadmium](/wiki/Cadmium "Cadmium") (element 48) isotopes.[28]

The palladium [isotope](/wiki/Isotope "Isotope") 107Pd decays by beta emission to 107Ag with a half-life of 6.5 million years. [Iron meteorites](/wiki/Iron_meteorite "Iron meteorite") are the only objects with a high-enough palladium-to-silver ratio to yield measurable variations in 107Ag abundance. [Radiogenic](/wiki/Radiogenic "Radiogenic") 107Ag was first discovered in the [Santa Clara](/wiki/Santa_Clara,_Durango "Santa Clara, Durango") meteorite in 1978.[30] 107Pd–107Ag correlations observed in bodies that have clearly been melted since the [accretion](/wiki/Accretion_\(astrophysics\) "Accretion \(astrophysics\)") of the [Solar System](/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System") must reflect the presence of unstable nuclides in the early Solar System.[31]

Silver is a rather unreactive metal. This is because its filled 4d shell is not very effective in shielding the electrostatic forces of attraction from the nucleus to the outermost 5s electron, and hence silver is near the bottom of the [electrochemical series](/wiki/Electrochemical_series "Electrochemical series") (_E_ 0(Ag+/Ag) = +0.799 V).[15] In group 11, silver has the lowest first ionisation energy (showing the instability of the 5s orbital), but has higher second and third ionisation energies than copper and gold (showing the stability of the 4d orbitals), so that the chemistry of silver is predominantly that of the +1 oxidation state, reflecting the increasingly limited range of oxidation states along the transition series as the d-orbitals fill and stabilise.[33] Unlike [copper](/wiki/Copper "Copper"), for which the larger [hydration energy](/wiki/Hydration_energy "Hydration energy") of Cu2+ as compared to Cu+ is the reason why the former is the more stable in aqueous solution and solids despite lacking the stable filled d-subshell of the latter, with silver this effect is swamped by its larger second ionisation energy. Hence, Ag+ is the stable species in aqueous solution and solids, with Ag2+ being much less stable as it oxidises water.[33]

Most silver compounds have significant [covalent](/wiki/Covalent_bond "Covalent bond") character due to the small size and high first ionisation energy (730.8 kJ/mol) of silver.[15] Furthermore, silver's Pauling [electronegativity](/wiki/Electronegativity "Electronegativity") of 1.93 is higher than that of [lead](/wiki/Lead "Lead") (1.87), and its [electron affinity](/wiki/Electron_affinity "Electron affinity") of 125.6 kJ/mol is much higher than that of [hydrogen](/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") (72.8 kJ/mol) and not much less than that of [oxygen](/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen") (141.0 kJ/mol).[34] Due to its full d-subshell, silver in its main +1 oxidation state exhibits relatively few properties of the transition metals proper from groups 4 to 10, forming rather unstable [organometallic compounds](/wiki/Organometallic_compound "Organometallic compound"), forming linear complexes showing very low [coordination numbers](/wiki/Coordination_number "Coordination number") like 2, and forming an amphoteric oxide[35] as well as [Zintl phases](/wiki/Zintl_phase "Zintl phase") like the [post-transition metals](/wiki/Post-transition_metal "Post-transition metal").[36] Unlike the preceding transition metals, the +1 oxidation state of silver is stable even in the absence of [π-acceptor ligands](/wiki/Pi_backbonding "Pi backbonding").[33]

Silver does not react with air, even at red heat, and thus was considered by [alchemists](/wiki/Alchemist "Alchemist") to be a [noble metal](/wiki/Noble_metal "Noble metal"). Its reactivity is intermediate between that of copper (which forms [copper(I) oxide](/wiki/Copper\(I\)_oxide "Copper\(I\) oxide") when heated in air to red heat) and gold. Like copper, silver reacts with [sulfur](/wiki/Sulfur "Sulfur") and its compounds; in their presence, silver tarnishes in air to form the black [silver sulfide](/wiki/Silver_sulfide "Silver sulfide") (copper forms the green [sulfate](/wiki/Sulfate "Sulfate") instead, while gold does not react). While silver is not attacked by non-oxidising acids, the metal dissolves readily in hot concentrated [sulfuric acid](/wiki/Sulfuric_acid "Sulfuric acid"), as well as dilute or concentrated [nitric acid](/wiki/Nitric_acid "Nitric acid"). In the presence of air, and especially in the presence of [hydrogen peroxide](/wiki/Hydrogen_peroxide "Hydrogen peroxide"), silver dissolves readily in aqueous solutions of [cyanide](/wiki/Cyanide "Cyanide").[32]

The three main forms of deterioration in historical silver artifacts are tarnishing, formation of [silver chloride](/wiki/Silver_chloride "Silver chloride") due to long-term immersion in salt water, as well as reaction with [nitrate](/wiki/Nitrate "Nitrate") ions or oxygen. Fresh silver chloride is pale yellow, becoming purplish on exposure to light; it projects slightly from the surface of the artifact or coin. The precipitation of copper in ancient silver can be used to date artifacts, as copper is nearly always a constituent of silver alloys.[37]

Silver metal is attacked by strong [oxidant](/wiki/Oxidizing_agent "Oxidizing agent") such as [potassium permanganate](/wiki/Potassium_permanganate "Potassium permanganate") (KMnO  
4) and [potassium dichromate](/wiki/Potassium_dichromate "Potassium dichromate") (K  
2Cr  
2O  
7), and in the presence of [potassium bromide](/wiki/Potassium_bromide "Potassium bromide") (KBr). These compounds are used in photography to [bleach](/wiki/Bleach "Bleach") silver images, converting them to silver bromide that can either be fixed with [thiosulfate](/wiki/Thiosulfate "Thiosulfate") or redeveloped to [intensify](/wiki/Potassium_dichromate#photography "Potassium dichromate") the original image. Silver forms [cyanide](/wiki/Cyanide "Cyanide") complexes ([silver cyanide](/wiki/Silver_cyanide "Silver cyanide")) that are soluble in water in the presence of an excess of cyanide ions. Silver cyanide solutions are used in [electroplating](/wiki/Electroplating "Electroplating") of silver.[38]

The common [oxidation states](/wiki/Oxidation_state "Oxidation state") of silver are (in order of commonness): +1 (the most stable state; for example, [silver nitrate](/wiki/Silver_nitrate "Silver nitrate"), AgNO3); +2 (highly oxidising; for example, [silver(II) fluoride](/wiki/Silver\(II\)_fluoride "Silver\(II\) fluoride"), AgF2); and even very rarely +3 (extreme oxidising; for example, potassium tetrafluoroargentate(III), KAgF4).[39] The +3 state requires very strong oxidising agents to attain, such as [fluorine](/wiki/Fluorine "Fluorine") or [peroxodisulfate](/wiki/Peroxodisulfate "Peroxodisulfate"), and some silver(III) compounds react with atmospheric moisture and attack glass.[40] Indeed, silver(III) fluoride is usually obtained by reacting silver or silver monofluoride with the strongest known oxidising agent, [krypton difluoride](/wiki/Krypton_difluoride "Krypton difluoride").[41]

## Compounds

### Oxides and chalcogenides

[](/wiki/File:Sulfid_st%C5%99%C3%ADbrn%C3%BD.PNG)Silver(I) sulfide

Silver and gold have rather low [chemical affinities](/wiki/Chemical_affinity "Chemical affinity") for oxygen, lower than copper, and it is therefore expected that silver oxides are thermally quite unstable. Soluble silver(I) salts precipitate dark-brown [silver(I) oxide](/wiki/Silver\(I\)_oxide "Silver\(I\) oxide"), Ag2O, upon the addition of alkali. (The hydroxide AgOH exists only in solution; otherwise it spontaneously decomposes to the oxide.) Silver(I) oxide is very easily reduced to metallic silver, and decomposes to silver and oxygen above 160 °C.[42] This and other silver(I) compounds may be oxidised by the strong oxidising agent [peroxodisulfate](/wiki/Peroxodisulfate "Peroxodisulfate") to black AgO, a mixed [silver(I,III) oxide](/wiki/Silver\(I,III\)_oxide "Silver\(I,III\) oxide") of formula AgIAgIIIO2. Some other mixed oxides with silver in non-integral oxidation states, namely Ag2O3 and Ag3O4, are also known, as is Ag3O which behaves as a metallic conductor.[42]

[Silver(I) sulfide](/wiki/Silver\(I\)_sulfide "Silver\(I\) sulfide"), Ag2S, is very readily formed from its constituent elements and is the cause of the black tarnish on some old silver objects. It may also be formed from the reaction of [hydrogen sulfide](/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide "Hydrogen sulfide") with silver metal or aqueous Ag+ ions. Many non-stoichiometric [selenides](/wiki/Selenide "Selenide") and [tellurides](/wiki/Telluride_\(chemistry\) "Telluride \(chemistry\)") are known; in particular, AgTe~3 is a low-temperature [superconductor](/wiki/Superconductor "Superconductor").[42]

### Halides

Main article: [Silver halide](/wiki/Silver_halide "Silver halide")

[](/wiki/File:Common_Silver_Halide_Precipitates.jpg)The three common silver halide precipitates: from left to right, [silver iodide](/wiki/Silver_iodide "Silver iodide"), [silver bromide](/wiki/Silver_bromide "Silver bromide"), and [silver chloride](/wiki/Silver_chloride "Silver chloride")

The only known dihalide of silver is [the difluoride](/wiki/Silver\(II\)_fluoride "Silver\(II\) fluoride"), AgF2, which can be obtained from the elements under heat. A strong yet thermally stable and therefore safe fluorinating agent, silver(II) fluoride is often used to synthesise [hydrofluorocarbons](/wiki/Hydrofluorocarbon "Hydrofluorocarbon").[43]

In stark contrast to this, all four silver(I) halides are known. The [fluoride](/wiki/Silver\(I\)_fluoride "Silver\(I\) fluoride"), [chloride](/wiki/Silver_chloride "Silver chloride"), and [bromide](/wiki/Silver_bromide "Silver bromide") have the sodium chloride structure, but the [iodide](/wiki/Silver_iodide "Silver iodide") has three known stable forms at different temperatures; that at room temperature is the cubic [zinc blende](/wiki/Zinc_blende "Zinc blende") structure. They can all be obtained by the direct reaction of their respective elements.[43] As the halogen group is descended, the silver halide gains more and more covalent character, solubility decreases, and the colour changes from the white chloride to the yellow iodide as the energy required for [ligand-metal charge transfer](/wiki/Charge-transfer_complex "Charge-transfer complex") (X−Ag+ → XAg) decreases.[43] The fluoride is anomalous, as the fluoride ion is so small that it has a considerable [solvation](/wiki/Solvation "Solvation") energy and hence is highly water-soluble and forms di- and tetrahydrates.[43] The other three silver halides are highly insoluble in aqueous solutions and are very commonly used in gravimetric [analytical](/wiki/Wet_chemistry "Wet chemistry") methods.[25] All four are [photosensitive](/wiki/Photosensitive "Photosensitive") (though the monofluoride is so only to [ultraviolet](/wiki/Ultraviolet "Ultraviolet") light), especially the bromide and iodide which photodecompose to silver metal, and thus were used in [traditional photography](/wiki/Monochrome_photography "Monochrome photography").[43] The reaction involved is:[44]

    X− \+ _hν_ → X + e− (excitation of the halide ion, which gives up its extra electron into the conduction band)
    Ag+ \+ e− → Ag (liberation of a silver ion, which gains an electron to become a silver atom)

The process is not reversible because the silver atom liberated is typically found at a [crystal defect](/wiki/Crystal_defect "Crystal defect") or an impurity site, so that the electron's energy is lowered enough that it is "trapped".[44]

### Other inorganic compounds

Silver crystals forming on a copper surface in a silver nitrate solution. Video by [Maxim Bilovitskiy](/wiki/Maxim_Bilovitskiy "Maxim Bilovitskiy").  [](/wiki/File:Silver_nitrate_crystals.jpg)Crystals of silver nitrate

White [silver nitrate](/wiki/Silver_nitrate "Silver nitrate"), AgNO3, is a versatile precursor to many other silver compounds, especially the halides, and is much less sensitive to light. It was once called _lunar caustic_ because silver was called _luna_ by the ancient alchemists, who believed that silver was associated with the Moon.[45][46] It is often used for gravimetric analysis, exploiting the insolubility of the heavier silver halides which it is a common precursor to.[25] Silver nitrate is used in many ways in [organic synthesis](/wiki/Organic_synthesis "Organic synthesis"), e.g. for [deprotection](/wiki/Deprotection "Deprotection") and oxidations. Ag+ binds [alkenes](/wiki/Alkene "Alkene") reversibly, and silver nitrate has been used to separate mixtures of alkenes by selective absorption. The resulting [adduct](/wiki/Adduct "Adduct") can be decomposed with [ammonia](/wiki/Ammonia "Ammonia") to release the free alkene.[47]

Yellow [silver carbonate](/wiki/Silver_carbonate "Silver carbonate"), Ag2CO3 can be easily prepared by reacting aqueous solutions of [sodium carbonate](/wiki/Sodium_carbonate "Sodium carbonate") with a deficiency of silver nitrate.[48] Its principal use is for the production of silver powder for use in microelectronics. It is reduced with [formaldehyde](/wiki/Formaldehyde "Formaldehyde"), producing silver free of alkali metals:[49]

    Ag2CO3 \+ CH2O → 2 Ag + 2 CO2 \+ H2

Silver carbonate is also used as a [reagent](/wiki/Reagent "Reagent") in organic synthesis such as the [Koenigs–Knorr reaction](/wiki/Koenigs%E2%80%93Knorr_reaction "Koenigs–Knorr reaction"). In the [Fétizon oxidation](/wiki/F%C3%A9tizon_oxidation "Fétizon oxidation"), silver carbonate on [celite](/wiki/Celite "Celite") acts as an [oxidising agent](/wiki/Oxidising_agent "Oxidising agent") to form [lactones](/wiki/Lactone "Lactone") from [diols](/wiki/Diols "Diols"). It is also employed to convert [alkyl](/wiki/Alkyl "Alkyl") bromides into [alcohols](/wiki/Alcohol_\(chemistry\) "Alcohol \(chemistry\)").[48]

[Silver fulminate](/wiki/Silver_fulminate "Silver fulminate"), AgCNO, a powerful, touch-sensitive [explosive](/wiki/Explosive "Explosive") used in [percussion caps](/wiki/Percussion_cap "Percussion cap"), is made by reaction of silver metal with nitric acid in the presence of [ethanol](/wiki/Ethanol "Ethanol"). Other dangerously explosive silver compounds are [silver azide](/wiki/Silver_azide "Silver azide"), AgN3, formed by reaction of silver nitrate with [sodium azide](/wiki/Sodium_azide "Sodium azide"),[50] and [silver acetylide](/wiki/Silver_acetylide "Silver acetylide"), Ag2C2, formed when silver reacts with [acetylene](/wiki/Acetylene "Acetylene") gas in ammonia solution.[33] In its most characteristic reaction, silver azide decomposes explosively, releasing nitrogen gas: given the photosensitivity of silver salts, this behaviour may be induced by shining a light on its crystals.[33]

### Coordination compounds

[](/wiki/File:Diamminesilver\(I\)-3D-balls.png)Structure of the diamminesilver(I) complex, [Ag(NH3)2]+

Silver complexes tend to be similar to those of its lighter homologue copper. Silver(III) complexes tend to be rare and very easily reduced to the more stable lower oxidation states, though they are slightly more stable than those of copper(III). For instance, the square planar periodate [Ag(IO5OH)2]5− and tellurate [Ag{TeO4(OH)2}2]5− complexes may be prepared by oxidising silver(I) with alkaline [peroxodisulfate](/wiki/Peroxodisulfate "Peroxodisulfate"). The yellow diamagnetic [AgF4]− is much less stable, fuming in moist air and reacting with glass.[40]

Silver(II) complexes are more common. Like the valence isoelectronic copper(II) complexes, they are usually square planar and paramagnetic, which is increased by the greater field splitting for 4d electrons than for 3d electrons. Aqueous Ag2+, produced by oxidation of Ag+ by ozone, is a very strong oxidising agent, even in acidic solutions: it is stabilised in [phosphoric acid](/wiki/Phosphoric_acid "Phosphoric acid") due to complex formation. Peroxodisulfate oxidation is generally necessary to give the more stable complexes with heterocyclic [amines](/wiki/Amine "Amine"), such as [Ag(py)4]2+ and [Ag(bipy)2]2+: these are stable provided the counterion cannot reduce the silver back to the +1 oxidation state. [AgF4]2− is also known in its violet barium salt, as are some silver(II) complexes with _N_ \- or _O_ -donor ligands such as pyridine carboxylates.[51]

By far the most important oxidation state for silver in complexes is +1. The Ag+ cation is diamagnetic, like its homologues Cu+ and Au+, as all three have closed-shell electron configurations with no unpaired electrons: its complexes are colourless provided the ligands are not too easily polarised such as I−. Ag+ forms salts with most anions, but it is reluctant to coordinate to oxygen and thus most of these salts are insoluble in water: the exceptions are the nitrate, perchlorate, and fluoride. The tetracoordinate tetrahedral aqueous ion [Ag(H2O)4]+ is known, but the characteristic geometry for the Ag+ cation is 2-coordinate linear. For example, silver chloride dissolves readily in excess aqueous ammonia to form [Ag(NH3)2]+; silver salts are dissolved in photography due to the formation of the thiosulfate complex [Ag(S2O3)2]3−; and [cyanide](/wiki/Cyanide "Cyanide") extraction for silver (and gold) works by the formation of the complex [Ag(CN)2]−. Silver cyanide forms the linear polymer {Ag–C≡N→Ag–C≡N→}; silver [thiocyanate](/wiki/Thiocyanate "Thiocyanate") has a similar structure, but forms a zigzag instead because of the sp3-[hybridized](/wiki/Orbital_hybridization "Orbital hybridization") sulfur atom. [Chelating ligands](/wiki/Chelating_ligand "Chelating ligand") are unable to form linear complexes and thus silver(I) complexes with them tend to form polymers; a few exceptions exist, such as the near-tetrahedral [diphosphine](/wiki/Diphosphine "Diphosphine") and [diarsine](/wiki/Diarsine "Diarsine") complexes [Ag(L–L)2]+.[52]

### Organometallic

Main article: [Organosilver chemistry](/wiki/Organosilver_chemistry "Organosilver chemistry")

Under standard conditions, silver does not form simple carbonyls, due to the weakness of the Ag–C bond. A few are known at very low temperatures around 6–15 K, such as the green, planar paramagnetic Ag(CO)3, which dimerises at 25–30 K, probably by forming Ag–Ag bonds. Additionally, the silver carbonyl [Ag(CO)][B(OTeF5)4] is known. Polymeric AgLX complexes with [alkenes](/wiki/Alkene "Alkene") and [alkynes](/wiki/Alkyne "Alkyne") are known, but their bonds are thermodynamically weaker than even those of the [platinum](/wiki/Platinum "Platinum") complexes (though they are formed more readily than those of the analogous gold complexes): they are also quite unsymmetrical, showing the weak _π_ bonding in group 11. Ag–C _σ_ bonds may also be formed by silver(I), like copper(I) and gold(I), but the simple alkyls and aryls of silver(I) are even less stable than those of copper(I) (which tend to explode under ambient conditions). For example, poor thermal stability is reflected in the relative decomposition temperatures of AgMe (−50 °C) and CuMe (−15 °C) as well as those of PhAg (74 °C) and PhCu (100 °C).[53]

The C–Ag bond is stabilised by [perfluoroalkyl](/wiki/Perfluoroalkane "Perfluoroalkane") ligands, for example in AgCF(CF3)2.[54] Alkenylsilver compounds are also more stable than their alkylsilver counterparts.[55] Silver-[NHC complexes](/wiki/Transition_metal_carbene_complex "Transition metal carbene complex") are easily prepared, and are commonly used to prepare other NHC complexes by displacing labile ligands. For example, the reaction of the bis(NHC)silver(I) complex with [bis(acetonitrile)palladium dichloride](/wiki/Bis\(acetonitrile\)palladium_dichloride "Bis\(acetonitrile\)palladium dichloride") or [chlorido(dimethyl sulfide)gold(I)](/wiki/Chlorido\(dimethyl_sulfide\)gold\(I\) "Chlorido\(dimethyl sulfide\)gold\(I\)"):[56]

### Intermetallic

[](/wiki/File:Ag-Au-Cu-colours-english.svg)Different colors of silver–copper–gold alloys

Silver forms [alloys](/wiki/Alloy "Alloy") with most other elements on the periodic table. The elements from groups 1–3, except for [hydrogen](/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen"), [lithium](/wiki/Lithium "Lithium"), and [beryllium](/wiki/Beryllium "Beryllium"), are very miscible with silver in the condensed phase and form intermetallic compounds; those from groups 4–9 are only poorly miscible; the elements in groups 10–14 (except [boron](/wiki/Boron "Boron") and [carbon](/wiki/Carbon "Carbon")) have very complex Ag–M phase diagrams and form the most commercially important alloys; and the remaining elements on the periodic table have no consistency in their Ag–M phase diagrams. By far the most important such alloys are those with copper: most silver used for coinage and jewellery is in reality a silver–copper alloy, and the [eutectic mixture](/wiki/Eutectic_mixture "Eutectic mixture") is used in vacuum [brazing](/wiki/Brazing "Brazing"). The two metals are completely miscible as liquids but not as solids; their importance in industry comes from the fact that their properties tend to be suitable over a wide range of variation in silver and copper concentration, although most useful alloys tend to be richer in silver than the eutectic mixture (71.9% silver and 28.1% copper by weight, and 60.1% silver and 28.1% copper by atom).[57]

Most other binary alloys are of little use: for example, silver–gold alloys are too soft and silver–[cadmium](/wiki/Cadmium "Cadmium") alloys too toxic. Ternary alloys have much greater importance: dental [amalgams](/wiki/Amalgam_\(dentistry\) "Amalgam \(dentistry\)") are usually silver–tin–mercury alloys, silver–copper–gold alloys are very important in jewellery (usually on the gold-rich side) and have a vast range of hardnesses and colours, silver–copper–zinc alloys are useful as low-melting brazing alloys, and silver–cadmium–[indium](/wiki/Indium "Indium") (involving three adjacent elements on the periodic table) is useful in [nuclear reactors](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor") because of its high thermal neutron capture [cross-section](/wiki/Cross_section_\(physics\) "Cross section \(physics\)"), good conduction of heat, mechanical stability, and resistance to corrosion in hot water.[57]

## Etymology

The word _silver_ appears in [Old English](/wiki/Old_English "Old English") with various spellings, such as _seolfor_ and _siolfor_. Its [cognates](/wiki/Cognate "Cognate") include [Old High German](/wiki/Old_High_German "Old High German") _silabar_ , [Gothic](/wiki/Gothic_language "Gothic language") _silubr_ and [Old Norse](/wiki/Old_Norse "Old Norse") _silfr_ , all ultimately derived from [Proto-Germanic](/wiki/Proto-Germanic_language "Proto-Germanic language") *_silubrą_. The [Balto-Slavic](/wiki/Balto-Slavic_languages "Balto-Slavic languages") words for silver resemble those of the Germanic branch (e.g. [Russian](/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language") серебро́ _serebró_ , [Polish](/wiki/Polish_language "Polish language") _srebro_ and [Lithuanian](/wiki/Lithuanian_language "Lithuanian language") _sidãbras_), and so does the [Celtiberian](/wiki/Celtiberian_language "Celtiberian language") term _silabur_ 'money'. They could have a common Indo-European origin, although their morphology rather suggests a non-Indo-European _[Wanderwort](/wiki/Wanderwort "Wanderwort")_.[58][59] Some scholars have thus proposed a [Paleo-Hispanic](/wiki/Paleo-Hispanic_languages "Paleo-Hispanic languages") origin, pointing to [Basque](/wiki/Basque_language "Basque language") _zilar_ as evidence, though this remains speculative.[60]

The chemical symbol Ag is from the [Latin](/wiki/Latin "Latin") word for silver, _argentum_ , from the [Proto-Indo-European](/wiki/Proto-Indo-European "Proto-Indo-European") root *_h₂erǵ-_ , meaning 'white' or 'shining'. This was the usual PIE term for the metal, whose reflexes are missing in Germanic and Balto-Slavic.[a][59]

## History

[](/wiki/File:Vase_Entemena_Louvre_AO2674.jpg)Silver vase, c. 2400 BC [](/wiki/File:Karashamb_goblet.jpg)[Karashamb silver goblet](/wiki/Karashamb_goblet "Karashamb goblet"), 23rd–22nd century BC

Silver was known in prehistoric times:[62] the three metals of group 11, copper, silver, and gold, occur in the [elemental form](/wiki/Native_metal "Native metal") in nature and were probably used as the first primitive forms of [money](/wiki/Money "Money") as opposed to simple bartering.[63] Unlike copper, silver did not lead to the growth of [metallurgy](/wiki/Metallurgy "Metallurgy"), on account of its low structural strength; it was more often used ornamentally or as money.[64] Since silver is more reactive than gold, supplies of [native silver](/wiki/Native_silver "Native silver") were much more limited than those of gold.[63] For example, silver was more expensive than gold in Egypt until around the fifteenth century BC:[65] the Egyptians are thought to have separated gold from silver by heating the metals with salt, and then reducing the [silver chloride](/wiki/Silver_chloride "Silver chloride") produced to the metal.[66]

The situation changed with the discovery of [cupellation](/wiki/Cupellation "Cupellation"), a technique that allowed silver metal to be extracted from its ores. While [slag](/wiki/Slag "Slag") heaps found in [Asia Minor](/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor") and on the islands of the [Aegean Sea](/wiki/Aegean_Sea "Aegean Sea") indicate that silver was being separated from [lead](/wiki/Lead "Lead") as early as the [4th millennium BC](/wiki/4th_millennium_BC "4th millennium BC"),[14] and one of the earliest silver extraction centres in Europe was [Sardinia](/wiki/Sardinia "Sardinia") in the early [Chalcolithic period](/wiki/Chalcolithic_period "Chalcolithic period"),[67] these techniques did not spread widely until later, when it spread throughout the region and beyond.[65] The origins of silver production in [India](/wiki/India "India"), [China](/wiki/China "China"), and [Japan](/wiki/Japan "Japan") were almost certainly equally ancient, but are not well-documented due to their great age.[66]

[](/wiki/File:Silver_mining_in_Kutn%C3%A1_Hora_1490s.jpg)Silver mining and processing in [Kutná Hora](/wiki/Kutn%C3%A1_Hora "Kutná Hora"), Bohemia, 1490s

When the [Phoenicians](/wiki/Phoenicia "Phoenicia") first came to what is now [Spain](/wiki/Spain "Spain"), they obtained so much silver that they could not fit it all on their ships, and as a result used silver to weight their anchors instead of lead.[65] By the time of the Greek and Roman civilisations, silver coins were a staple of the economy:[63] the Greeks were already extracting silver from [galena](/wiki/Galena "Galena") by the 7th century BC,[65] and the rise of [Athens](/wiki/Athens "Athens") was partly made possible by the nearby silver mines at [Laurium](/wiki/Laurium "Laurium"), from which they extracted about 30 tonnes a year from 600 to 300 BC.[68] The stability of the [Roman currency](/wiki/Roman_currency "Roman currency") relied to a high degree on the supply of silver bullion, mostly from Spain, which [Roman miners](/wiki/Roman_metallurgy "Roman metallurgy") produced on a scale unparalleled before the [discovery of the New World](/wiki/Discovery_of_the_New_World "Discovery of the New World"). Reaching a peak production of 200 tonnes per year, an estimated silver stock of 10,000 tonnes circulated in the [Roman economy](/wiki/Roman_economy "Roman economy") in the middle of the second century AD, five to ten times larger than the combined amount of silver available to [medieval Europe](/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages "Early Middle Ages") and the [Abbasid Caliphate](/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate "Abbasid Caliphate") around AD 800.[69][70] The Romans also recorded the extraction of silver in central and northern Europe in the same time period. This production came to a nearly complete halt with the fall of the Roman Empire, not to resume until the time of [Charlemagne](/wiki/Charlemagne "Charlemagne"): by then, tens of thousands of tonnes of silver had already been extracted.[66]

Central Europe became the centre of silver production during the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), as the Mediterranean deposits exploited by the ancient civilisations had been exhausted. Silver mines were opened in [Bohemia](/wiki/Bohemia "Bohemia"), [Saxony](/wiki/Saxony "Saxony"), [Alsace](/wiki/Alsace "Alsace"), the [Lahn](/wiki/Lahn "Lahn") region, [Siegerland](/wiki/Siegerland "Siegerland"), [Silesia](/wiki/Silesia "Silesia"), [Hungary](/wiki/Hungary "Hungary"), [Norway](/wiki/Norway "Norway"), [Steiermark](/wiki/Steiermark "Steiermark"), [Schwaz](/wiki/Schwaz "Schwaz"), and the southern [Black Forest](/wiki/Black_Forest "Black Forest"). Most of these ores were quite rich in silver and could simply be separated by hand from the remaining rock and then smelted; some deposits of [native silver](/wiki/Native_silver "Native silver") were also encountered. Many of these mines were soon exhausted, but a few of them remained active until the [Industrial Revolution](/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution"), before which the world production of silver was around a meagre 50 tonnes per year.[66] In the Americas, high temperature silver-lead [cupellation](/wiki/Cupellation "Cupellation") technology was developed by pre-Inca civilisations as early as AD 60–120; silver deposits in India, China, Japan, and pre-Columbian America continued to be mined during this time.[66][71]

With the discovery of America and the plundering of silver by the Spanish conquistadors, Central and South America became the dominant producers of silver until around the beginning of the 18th century, particularly [Peru](/wiki/Peru "Peru"), [Bolivia](/wiki/Bolivia "Bolivia"), [Chile](/wiki/Chile "Chile"), and [Argentina](/wiki/Argentina "Argentina"):[66] the last of these countries later took its name from that of the metal that composed so much of its mineral wealth.[68] The silver trade gave way to a [global network of exchange](/wiki/Global_silver_trade_from_the_16th_to_19th_centuries "Global silver trade from the 16th to 19th centuries"). As one historian put it, silver "went round the world and made the world go round."[72] Much of this silver ended up in the hands of the Chinese. A Portuguese merchant in 1621 noted that silver "wanders throughout all the world... before flocking to China, where it remains as if at its natural centre".[73] Still, much of it went to Spain, allowing Spanish rulers to pursue military and political ambitions in both Europe and the Americas. "New World mines", concluded several historians, "supported the Spanish empire."[74]

In the 19th century, primary production of silver moved to North America, particularly [Canada](/wiki/Canada "Canada"), [Mexico](/wiki/Mexico "Mexico"), and [Nevada](/wiki/Nevada "Nevada") in the [United States](/wiki/United_States "United States"): some secondary production from lead and zinc ores also took place in Europe, and deposits in [Siberia](/wiki/Siberia "Siberia") and the [Russian Far East](/wiki/Russian_Far_East "Russian Far East") as well as in [Australia](/wiki/Australia "Australia") were mined.[66] [Poland](/wiki/Poland "Poland") emerged as an important producer during the 1970s after the discovery of copper deposits that were rich in silver, before the centre of production returned to the Americas the following decade. Today, Peru and Mexico are still among the primary silver producers, but the distribution of silver production around the world is quite balanced and about one-fifth of the silver supply comes from recycling instead of new production.[66]

## Symbolic role

[](/wiki/File:6852_les_deniers_de_judas.JPG)16th-century fresco painting of Judas being paid thirty pieces of silver for his betrayal of Jesus

Silver plays a certain role in mythology and has found various usage as a metaphor and in folklore. The Greek poet [Hesiod](/wiki/Hesiod "Hesiod")'s _[Works and Days](/wiki/Works_and_Days "Works and Days")_ (lines 109–201) lists different [ages of man](/wiki/Ages_of_man "Ages of man") named after metals like gold, silver, bronze and iron to account for successive ages of humanity.[75] [Ovid](/wiki/Ovid "Ovid")'s _[Metamorphoses](/wiki/Metamorphoses "Metamorphoses")_ contains another retelling of the story, containing an illustration of silver's metaphorical use of signifying the second-best in a series, better than bronze but worse than gold: 

> But when good [Saturn](/wiki/Saturn_\(mythology\) "Saturn \(mythology\)"), banish'd from above,  
>  Was driv'n to Hell, the world was under [Jove](/wiki/Jupiter_\(mythology\) "Jupiter \(mythology\)").  
>  Succeeding times a silver age behold,  
>  Excelling brass, but more excell'd by gold. 

— Ovid, [Metamorphoses](/wiki/Metamorphoses "Metamorphoses"), Book I, trans. [John Dryden](/wiki/John_Dryden "John Dryden")

In folklore, silver was commonly thought to have mystic powers: for example, a [bullet cast from silver](/wiki/Silver_bullet "Silver bullet") is often supposed in such folklore the only weapon that is effective against a [werewolf](/wiki/Werewolf "Werewolf"), [witch](/wiki/Witch "Witch"), or other [monsters](/wiki/Monster "Monster").[76][77][78] From this the idiom of a silver bullet developed into figuratively referring to any simple solution with very high effectiveness or almost miraculous results, as in the widely discussed [software engineering](/wiki/Software_engineering "Software engineering") paper "[No Silver Bullet](/wiki/No_Silver_Bullet "No Silver Bullet")".[79] Other powers attributed to silver include detection of poison and facilitation of passage into the [mythical realm of fairies](/wiki/Fairyland "Fairyland").[78]

Silver production has also inspired figurative language. Clear references to cupellation occur throughout the [Old Testament](/wiki/Old_Testament "Old Testament") of the [Bible](/wiki/Bible "Bible"), such as in [Jeremiah](/wiki/Jeremiah "Jeremiah")'s rebuke to Judah: "The bellows are burned, the lead is consumed of the fire; the founder melteth in vain: for the wicked are not plucked away. Reprobate silver shall men call them, because the Lord hath rejected them." (Jeremiah 6:19–20) Jeremiah was also aware of sheet silver, exemplifying the malleability and ductility of the metal: "Silver spread into plates is brought from Tarshish, and gold from Uphaz, the work of the workman, and of the hands of the founder: blue and purple is their clothing: they are all the work of cunning men." (Jeremiah 10:9)[65]

Silver also has more negative cultural meanings: the idiom [thirty pieces of silver](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/thirty_pieces_of_silver "wikt:thirty pieces of silver"), referring to a reward for betrayal, references the bribe [Judas Iscariot](/wiki/Judas_Iscariot "Judas Iscariot") is said in the [New Testament](/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament") to have taken from Jewish leaders in [Jerusalem](/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem") to turn [Jesus of Nazareth](/wiki/Jesus "Jesus") over to soldiers of the high priest Caiaphas.[80] Ethically, silver also symbolizes greed and degradation of consciousness; this is the negative aspect, the perverting of its value.[81]

## Occurrence and production

Further information: [Silver mining](/wiki/Silver_mining "Silver mining")

[](/wiki/File:Silver_-_world_production_trend.svg)World production of silver

The abundance of silver in the Earth's crust is 0.08 [parts per million](/wiki/Parts_per_million "Parts per million"), almost exactly the same as that of [mercury](/wiki/Mercury_\(element\) "Mercury \(element\)"). It mostly occurs in [sulfide](/wiki/Sulfide "Sulfide") ores, especially [acanthite](/wiki/Acanthite "Acanthite") and [argentite](/wiki/Argentite "Argentite"), Ag2S. Argentite deposits sometimes also contain [native silver](/wiki/Native_silver "Native silver") when they occur in reducing environments, and when in contact with salt water they are converted to [chlorargyrite](/wiki/Chlorargyrite "Chlorargyrite") (including [horn silver](/wiki/Horn_silver "Horn silver")), AgCl, which is prevalent in [Chile](/wiki/Chile "Chile") and [New South Wales](/wiki/New_South_Wales "New South Wales").[82] Most other silver minerals are silver [pnictides](/wiki/Pnictide "Pnictide") or [chalcogenides](/wiki/Chalcogenide "Chalcogenide"); they are generally lustrous semiconductors. Most true silver deposits, as opposed to argentiferous deposits of other metals, came from [Tertiary](/wiki/Tertiary_\(period\) "Tertiary \(period\)") vulcanism.[83]

The principal sources of silver are the ores of copper, copper-nickel, lead, and lead-zinc obtained from [Peru](/wiki/Peru "Peru"), [Bolivia](/wiki/Bolivia "Bolivia"), [Mexico](/wiki/Mexico "Mexico"), [China](/wiki/China "China"), [Australia](/wiki/Australia "Australia"), [Chile](/wiki/Chile "Chile"), [Poland](/wiki/Poland "Poland") and [Serbia](/wiki/Serbia "Serbia").[14] Peru, Bolivia and Mexico have been mining silver since 1546, and are still major world producers. Top silver-producing mines are [Cannington](/wiki/Cannington_Mine "Cannington Mine") (Australia), [Fresnillo](/wiki/Mina_Proa%C3%B1o "Mina Proaño") (Mexico), [San Cristóbal](/wiki/San_Crist%C3%B3bal_mine_\(Bolivia\) "San Cristóbal mine \(Bolivia\)") (Bolivia), [Antamina](/wiki/Antamina_mine "Antamina mine") (Peru), [Rudna](/wiki/Rudna_mine "Rudna mine") (Poland), and [Penasquito](/wiki/Pe%C3%B1asquito_Polymetallic_Mine "Peñasquito Polymetallic Mine") (Mexico).[84] Top near-term mine development projects through 2015 are Pascua Lama (Chile), Navidad (Argentina), Jaunicipio (Mexico), Malku Khota (Bolivia),[85] and Hackett River (Canada).[84] In [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"), [Tajikistan](/wiki/Mining_in_Tajikistan#Silver "Mining in Tajikistan") is known to have some of the largest silver deposits in the world.[86]

Silver is usually found in nature combined with other metals, or in minerals that contain silver compounds, generally in the form of [sulfides](/wiki/Sulfides "Sulfides") such as [galena](/wiki/Galena "Galena") (lead sulfide) or [cerussite](/wiki/Cerussite "Cerussite") (lead carbonate). So the primary production of silver requires the smelting and then [cupellation](/wiki/Cupellation "Cupellation") of argentiferous lead ores, a historically important process.[87] Lead melts at 327 °C, lead oxide at 888 °C and silver melts at 960 °C. To separate the silver, the alloy is melted again at the high temperature of 960 °C to 1000 °C in an oxidising environment. The lead oxidises to [lead monoxide](/wiki/Lead\(II\)_oxide "Lead\(II\) oxide"), then known as [litharge](/wiki/Litharge "Litharge"), which captures the oxygen from the other metals present. The liquid lead oxide is removed or absorbed by [capillary action](/wiki/Capillary_action "Capillary action") into the hearth linings.[88][89][90]

    Ag(s) + 2Pb(s) + O  
2(g) → 2PbO(absorbed) + Ag(l)

Today, silver metal is primarily produced instead as a secondary byproduct of [electrolytic refining](/wiki/Refining_\(metallurgy\)#Electrolytic_refining "Refining \(metallurgy\)") of copper, lead, and zinc, and by application of the [Parkes process](/wiki/Parkes_process "Parkes process") on lead bullion from ore that also contains silver.[91] In such processes, silver follows the non-ferrous metal in question through its concentration and smelting, and is later purified out. For example, in copper production, purified copper is [electrolytically](/wiki/Electrolysis "Electrolysis") deposited on the cathode, while the less reactive precious metals such as silver and gold collect under the anode as the so-called "anode slime". This is then separated and purified of base metals by treatment with hot aerated dilute [sulfuric](/wiki/Sulfuric "Sulfuric") acid and heating with lime or silica flux, before the silver is purified to over 99.9% purity via electrolysis in [nitrate](/wiki/Nitrate "Nitrate") solution.[82]

Commercial-grade fine silver is at least 99.9% pure, and purities greater than 99.999% are available. In 2022, Mexico was the top producer of silver (6,300 [tonnes](/wiki/Tonne "Tonne") or 24.2% of the world's total of 26,000 t), followed by China (3,600 t) and Peru (3,100 t).[91]

### In marine environments

Silver concentration is low in [seawater](/wiki/Seawater "Seawater") (pmol/L). Levels vary by depth and between water bodies. Dissolved silver concentrations range from 0.3 pmol/L in coastal surface waters to 22.8 pmol/L in pelagic deep waters.[92] Analysing the presence and dynamics of silver in marine environments is difficult due to these particularly low concentrations and complex interactions in the environment.[93] Although a rare trace metal, concentrations are greatly impacted by fluvial, aeolian, atmospheric, and upwelling inputs, as well as anthropogenic inputs via discharge, waste disposal, and emissions from industrial companies.[94][95] Other internal processes such as decomposition of organic matter may be a source of dissolved silver in deeper waters, which feeds into some surface waters through upwelling and vertical mixing.[95]

In the Atlantic and Pacific, silver concentrations are minimal at the surface but rise in deeper waters.[96] Silver is taken up by plankton in the photic zone, remobilized with depth, and enriched in deep waters. Silver is transported from the Atlantic to the other oceanic water masses.[94] In North Pacific waters, silver is remobilised at a slower rate and increasingly enriched compared to deep Atlantic waters. Silver has increasing concentrations that follow the major oceanic conveyor belt that cycles water and nutrients from the North Atlantic to the South Atlantic to the North Pacific.[97]

There is not an extensive amount of data focused on how marine life is affected by silver despite the likely deleterious effects it could have on organisms through [bioaccumulation](/wiki/Bioaccumulation "Bioaccumulation"), association with particulate matters, and [sorption](/wiki/Sorption "Sorption").[92] Not until about 1984 did scientists begin to understand the chemical characteristics of silver and the potential toxicity. In fact, [mercury](/wiki/Mercury_\(element\) "Mercury \(element\)") is the only other trace metal that surpasses the toxic effects of silver; the full silver toxicity extent is not expected in oceanic conditions because of its tendency to transfer into nonreactive biological compounds.[98]

In one study, the presence of excess ionic silver and silver [nanoparticles](/wiki/Nanoparticle "Nanoparticle") caused bioaccumulation effects on zebrafish organs and altered the chemical pathways within their gills.[99] In addition, very early experimental studies demonstrated how the toxic effects of silver fluctuate with salinity and other parameters, as well as between life stages and different species such as finfish, molluscs, and crustaceans.[100] Another study found raised concentrations of silver in the muscles and liver of dolphins and whales, indicating pollution of this metal within recent decades. Silver is not an easy metal for an organism to eliminate and elevated concentrations can cause death.[101]

## Monetary use

[](/wiki/File:2022-american-eagle-silver-one-ounce-bullion-coin-obverse.png)An [American Silver Eagle](/wiki/American_Silver_Eagle "American Silver Eagle") bullion coin, minted from .999 fine silver

The earliest known coins were minted in the kingdom of [Lydia](/wiki/Lydia "Lydia") in [Asia Minor](/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") around 600 BC.[102] The coins of Lydia were made of [electrum](/wiki/Electrum "Electrum"), which is a naturally occurring [alloy](/wiki/Alloy "Alloy") of gold and silver, that was available within the territory of Lydia.[102] Since that time, [silver standards](/wiki/Silver_standard "Silver standard"), in which the standard economic [unit of account](/wiki/Unit_of_account "Unit of account") is a fixed weight of silver, have been widespread throughout the world until the 20th century. Notable [silver coins](/wiki/Silver_coin "Silver coin") through the centuries include the [Greek drachma](/wiki/Modern_drachma "Modern drachma"),[103] the Roman [denarius](/wiki/Denarius "Denarius"),[104] the Islamic [dirham](/wiki/Dirham "Dirham"),[105] the [karshapana](/wiki/Karshapana "Karshapana") from ancient India and [rupee](/wiki/Rupee "Rupee") from the time of the [Mughal Empire](/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") (grouped with copper and gold coins to create a trimetallic standard),[106] and the [Spanish dollar](/wiki/Spanish_dollar "Spanish dollar").[107]

The ratio between the amount of silver used for coinage and that used for other purposes has fluctuated greatly over time; for example, in wartime, more silver tends to have been used for coinage to finance the war.[108]

Today, silver bullion has the [ISO 4217](/wiki/ISO_4217 "ISO 4217") currency code XAG, one of only four [precious metals](/wiki/Precious_metal "Precious metal") to have one (the others being [platinum](/wiki/Platinum "Platinum"), [palladium](/wiki/Palladium "Palladium"), and gold).[109] Silver coins are produced from cast rods or ingots, rolled to the correct thickness, heat-treated, and then used to cut [blanks](/wiki/Planchet "Planchet") from. These blanks are then milled and minted in a coining press; modern coining presses can produce 8,000 silver coins per hour.[108]

### Price

[](/wiki/File:Price_of_silver.webp)Price of silver 1968–2025

See also: [Silver as an investment](/wiki/Silver_as_an_investment "Silver as an investment")

Silver prices are normally quoted in [troy ounces](/wiki/Troy_weight "Troy weight"). One troy ounce is equal to 31.1034768 grams. The London silver fix is published every working day at noon [London time](/wiki/London_time "London time").[110] This price is determined by several major international banks and is used by [London bullion market](/wiki/London_bullion_market "London bullion market") members for trading that day. Prices are most commonly shown as the [United States dollar](/wiki/United_States_dollar "United States dollar") (USD), the [Pound sterling](/wiki/Pound_sterling "Pound sterling") (GBP), and the [Euro](/wiki/Euro "Euro") (EUR). 

## Applications

### Jewellery and silverware

See also: [Silver plating](/wiki/Silver_plating "Silver plating"), [Silvering](/wiki/Silvering "Silvering"), and [Silver-gilt](/wiki/Silver-gilt "Silver-gilt")

[](/wiki/File:Rennen_Silver_sarcophagus_of_Saint_Stanislaus.jpg)[Embossed](/wiki/Repouss%C3%A9_and_chasing "Repoussé and chasing") silver sarcophagus of [Saint Stanislaus](/wiki/Stanislaus_of_Szczepan%C3%B3w "Stanislaus of Szczepanów") in the [Wawel Cathedral](/wiki/Wawel_Cathedral "Wawel Cathedral") was created in main centres of the 17th century European [silversmithery](/wiki/Silversmith "Silversmith") – [Augsburg](/wiki/Augsburg "Augsburg") and [Gdańsk](/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk "Gdańsk")[111] [](/wiki/File:L%C3%A4ck%C3%B6_slott_interior_42.jpg)17th-century silverware

The major use of silver besides coinage throughout most of history was in the manufacture of [jewellery](/wiki/Jewellery "Jewellery") and other general-use items, and this continues to be a major use today. Examples include [table silver](/wiki/Household_silver "Household silver") for cutlery, for which silver is highly suited due to its antibacterial properties. [Western concert flutes](/wiki/Western_concert_flute "Western concert flute") are usually plated with or made out of [sterling silver](/wiki/Sterling_silver "Sterling silver");[112] in fact, most silverware is only silver-plated rather than made out of pure silver; the silver is normally put in place by [electroplating](/wiki/Electroplating "Electroplating"). Silver-plated glass (as opposed to metal) is used for mirrors, [vacuum flasks](/wiki/Vacuum_flask "Vacuum flask"), and Christmas tree decorations.[113]

Because pure silver is very soft, most silver used for these purposes is alloyed with copper, with finenesses of 925/1000, 835/1000, and 800/1000 being common. One drawback is the easy tarnishing of silver in the presence of [hydrogen sulfide](/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide "Hydrogen sulfide") and its derivatives. Including precious metals such as palladium, platinum, and gold gives resistance to tarnishing but is quite costly; [base metals](/wiki/Base_metal "Base metal") like [zinc](/wiki/Zinc "Zinc"), [cadmium](/wiki/Cadmium "Cadmium"), [silicon](/wiki/Silicon "Silicon"), and [germanium](/wiki/Germanium "Germanium") do not totally prevent corrosion and tend to affect the lustre and colour of the alloy. Electrolytically refined pure silver plating is effective at increasing resistance to tarnishing. The usual solutions for restoring the lustre of tarnished silver are dipping baths that reduce the silver sulfide surface to metallic silver, and cleaning off the layer of tarnish with a paste; the latter approach also has the welcome side effect of polishing the silver concurrently.[112]

### Medicine

Main article: [Medical uses of silver](/wiki/Medical_uses_of_silver "Medical uses of silver")

In medicine, silver is incorporated into wound dressings and used as an antibiotic coating in medical devices. Wound dressings containing [silver sulfadiazine](/wiki/Silver_sulfadiazine "Silver sulfadiazine") or [silver nanomaterials](/wiki/Silver_nanoparticles "Silver nanoparticles") are used to treat external infections. Silver is also used in some medical applications, such as [urinary catheters](/wiki/Urinary_catheter "Urinary catheter") (where tentative evidence indicates it reduces catheter-related [urinary tract infections](/wiki/Urinary_tract_infections "Urinary tract infections")) and in [endotracheal breathing tubes](/wiki/Endotracheal_tube "Endotracheal tube") (where evidence suggests it reduces ventilator-associated [pneumonia](/wiki/Pneumonia "Pneumonia")).[114][115] The silver [ion](/wiki/Ion "Ion") is [bioactive](/wiki/Biological_activity "Biological activity") and in sufficient [concentration](/wiki/Concentration "Concentration") readily kills [bacteria](/wiki/Bacteria "Bacteria") _[in vitro](/wiki/In_vitro "In vitro")_. Silver ions interfere with enzymes in the bacteria that transport nutrients, form structures, and synthesise cell walls; these ions also bond with the bacteria's genetic material. Silver and silver nanoparticles are used as an antimicrobial in a variety of industrial, healthcare, and domestic application: for example, infusing clothing with nanosilver particles thus allows them to stay odourless for longer.[116][117] Bacteria can develop resistance to the antimicrobial action of silver.[118] Silver compounds are taken up by the body like [mercury](/wiki/Mercury_\(element\) "Mercury \(element\)") compounds, but lack the toxicity of the latter. Silver and its alloys are used in cranial surgery to replace bone, and silver–tin–mercury amalgams are used in dentistry.[113] [Silver diammine fluoride](/wiki/Silver_diammine_fluoride "Silver diammine fluoride"), the fluoride salt of a [coordination complex](/wiki/Coordination_complex "Coordination complex") with the formula [Ag(NH3)2]F, is a topical [medicament](/wiki/Medicament "Medicament") (drug) used to treat and prevent [dental caries](/wiki/Dental_caries "Dental caries") (cavities) and relieve dentinal hypersensitivity.[119]

### Electronics

See also: [Copper-clad aluminium wire](/wiki/Copper-clad_aluminium_wire "Copper-clad aluminium wire")

Silver is very important in electronics for conductors and electrodes on account of its high electrical conductivity even when tarnished. Bulk silver and silver foils were used to make vacuum tubes, and continue to be used today in the manufacture of semiconductor devices, circuits, and their components. For example, silver is used in high quality connectors for [RF](/wiki/Radio_frequency "Radio frequency"), [VHF](/wiki/Very_high_frequency "Very high frequency"), and higher frequencies, particularly in tuned circuits such as [cavity filters](/wiki/RF_and_microwave_filter#Cavity_filters "RF and microwave filter") where conductors cannot be scaled by more than 6%. [Printed circuits](/wiki/Printed_circuit_board "Printed circuit board") and [RFID](/wiki/RFID "RFID") antennas are made with silver paints,[14][120] Powdered silver and its alloys are used in paste preparations for conductor layers and electrodes, ceramic capacitors, and other ceramic components.[121]

### Brazing alloys

Silver-containing [brazing](/wiki/Brazing "Brazing") alloys are used for brazing metallic materials, mostly [cobalt](/wiki/Cobalt "Cobalt"), [nickel](/wiki/Nickel "Nickel"), and copper-based alloys, tool steels, and precious metals. The basic components are silver and copper, with other elements selected according to the specific application desired: examples include zinc, tin, cadmium, palladium, [manganese](/wiki/Manganese "Manganese"), and [phosphorus](/wiki/Phosphorus "Phosphorus"). Silver provides increased workability and corrosion resistance during usage.[122]

### Chemical equipment

Silver is useful in the manufacture of chemical equipment on account of its low chemical reactivity, high thermal conductivity, and being easily workable. Silver [crucibles](/wiki/Crucible "Crucible") (alloyed with 0.15% nickel to avoid recrystallisation of the metal at red heat) are used for carrying out alkaline fusion. Copper and silver are also used when doing chemistry with [fluorine](/wiki/Fluorine "Fluorine"). Equipment made to work at high temperatures is often silver-plated. Silver and its alloys with gold are used as wire or ring seals for oxygen compressors and vacuum equipment.[123]

### Catalysis

Silver metal is a good catalyst for [oxidation](/wiki/Oxidation "Oxidation") reactions; in fact it is somewhat too good for most purposes, as finely divided silver tends to result in complete oxidation of organic substances to [carbon dioxide](/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") and water, and hence coarser-grained silver tends to be used instead. For instance, 15% silver supported on α-Al2O3 or silicates is a catalyst for the oxidation of [ethylene](/wiki/Ethylene "Ethylene") to [ethylene oxide](/wiki/Ethylene_oxide "Ethylene oxide") at 230–270 °C. Dehydrogenation of [methanol](/wiki/Methanol "Methanol") to [formaldehyde](/wiki/Formaldehyde "Formaldehyde") is conducted at 600–720 °C over silver gauze or crystals as the catalyst, as is dehydrogenation of [isopropanol](/wiki/Isopropanol "Isopropanol") to [acetone](/wiki/Acetone "Acetone"). In the gas phase, [glycol](/wiki/Glycol "Glycol") yields [glyoxal](/wiki/Glyoxal "Glyoxal") and [ethanol](/wiki/Ethanol "Ethanol") yields [acetaldehyde](/wiki/Acetaldehyde "Acetaldehyde"), while organic [amines](/wiki/Amine "Amine") are dehydrated to [nitriles](/wiki/Nitrile "Nitrile").[123]

### Photography

Before the advent of [digital photography](/wiki/Digital_photography "Digital photography"), which is now dominant, the photosensitivity of silver halides was exploited for use in traditional film photography. The [photosensitive emulsion](/wiki/Photographic_emulsion "Photographic emulsion") used in black-and-white photography is a suspension of silver halide crystals in [gelatin](/wiki/Gelatin "Gelatin"), possibly mixed in with some noble metal compounds for improved photosensitivity, [developing](/wiki/Photographic_processing "Photographic processing"), and [print toning](/wiki/Photographic_print_toning "Photographic print toning").[_[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

[Colour photography](/wiki/Color_photography "Color photography") requires the addition of special dye components and sensitisers, so that the initial black-and-white silver image couples with a different dye component. The original silver images are bleached off and the silver is then recovered and recycled. Silver nitrate is the starting material in all cases.[124]

The market for silver nitrate and silver halides for photography has rapidly declined with the rise of digital cameras. From the peak global demand for photographic silver in 1999 (267,000,000 [troy ounces](/wiki/Troy_ounce "Troy ounce") or 8,304.6 [tonnes](/wiki/Tonne "Tonne")) the market contracted almost 70% by 2013.[125]

### Nanoparticles

Main article: [Silver nanoparticle](/wiki/Silver_nanoparticle "Silver nanoparticle")

Nanosilver particles, between 10 and 100 nanometres in size, are used in many applications. They are used in conductive inks for printed electronics, and have a much lower melting point than larger silver particles of micrometre size.[126] They are also used medicinally in antibacterials and antifungals in much the same way as larger silver particles.[117] In addition, according to the [European Union Observatory for Nanomaterials](/wiki/European_Union_Observatory_for_Nanomaterials "European Union Observatory for Nanomaterials") (EUON), silver nanoparticles are used both in pigments, as well as cosmetics.[127][128]

### Miscellanea

[](/wiki/File:Diwali_sweets_India_2009.jpg)A tray of [South Asian sweets](/wiki/South_Asian_sweets "South Asian sweets"), with some pieces covered with shiny silver _vark_

Pure silver metal is used as a food colouring. It has the [E174](/wiki/E_number "E number") designation and is approved in the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union "European Union").[129] Traditional Indian and Pakistani dishes sometimes include decorative silver foil known as _[vark](/wiki/Vark "Vark")_ ,[130] and in various other cultures, silver _[dragée](/wiki/Drag%C3%A9e "Dragée")_ are used to decorate cakes, cookies, and other dessert items.[131]

[Photochromic lenses](/wiki/Photochromic_lens "Photochromic lens") include silver halides, so that ultraviolet light in natural daylight liberates metallic silver, darkening the lenses. The silver halides are reformed in lower light intensities. Colourless silver chloride films are used in [radiation detectors](/wiki/Particle_detector "Particle detector"). [Zeolite](/wiki/Zeolite "Zeolite") sieves incorporating Ag+ ions are used to [desalinate](/wiki/Desalination "Desalination") seawater during rescues, using silver ions to precipitate chloride as silver chloride. Silver is also used for its antibacterial properties for water sanitisation, but the application of this is limited by limits on silver consumption. [Colloidal silver](/wiki/Colloidal_silver "Colloidal silver") is similarly used to disinfect closed swimming pools; while it has the advantage of not giving off a smell like [hypochlorite](/wiki/Hypochlorite "Hypochlorite") treatments do, colloidal silver is not effective enough for more contaminated open swimming pools. Small [silver iodide](/wiki/Silver_iodide "Silver iodide") crystals are used in [cloud seeding](/wiki/Cloud_seeding "Cloud seeding") to cause rain.[117]

The [Texas Legislature](/wiki/Texas_Legislature "Texas Legislature") designated silver the official precious metal of Texas in 2007.[132]

Chemical compound

Silver compounds have low toxicity compared to those of most other [heavy metals](/wiki/Heavy_metals "Heavy metals"), as they are poorly absorbed by the human body when ingested, and that which does get absorbed is rapidly converted to insoluble silver compounds or complexed by [metallothionein](/wiki/Metallothionein "Metallothionein"). Silver fluoride and silver nitrate are caustic and can cause tissue damage, resulting in [gastroenteritis](/wiki/Gastroenteritis "Gastroenteritis"), [diarrhoea](/wiki/Diarrhoea "Diarrhoea"), falling [blood pressure](/wiki/Blood_pressure "Blood pressure"), cramps, paralysis, or [respiratory arrest](/wiki/Respiratory_arrest "Respiratory arrest"). Animals repeatedly dosed with silver salts have been observed to experience [anaemia](/wiki/Anaemia "Anaemia"), slowed growth, [necrosis](/wiki/Necrosis "Necrosis") of the liver, and fatty degeneration of the liver and kidneys; rats implanted with silver foil or injected with [colloidal silver](/wiki/Colloidal_silver "Colloidal silver") have been observed to develop localised tumours. [Parenterally](/wiki/Route_of_administration "Route of administration") administered colloidal silver causes acute silver poisoning.[134] Some waterborne species are particularly sensitive to silver salts and those of the other precious metals; in most situations, silver is not a serious environmental hazard.[134]

In large doses, silver and compounds containing it can be absorbed into the [circulatory system](/wiki/Circulatory_system "Circulatory system") and become deposited in various body tissues, leading to [argyria](/wiki/Argyria "Argyria"), which results in a blue-grayish pigmentation of the skin, eyes, and [mucous membranes](/wiki/Mucous_membrane "Mucous membrane"). Argyria is rare, and so far as is known, does not otherwise harm a person's health, though it is disfiguring and usually permanent. Mild forms of argyria are sometimes mistaken for [cyanosis](/wiki/Cyanosis "Cyanosis"), a blue tint on skin, caused by lack of oxygen.[134][14]

Metallic silver, like copper, is an antibacterial agent, which was known to the ancients and first scientifically investigated and named the [oligodynamic effect](/wiki/Oligodynamic_effect "Oligodynamic effect") by [Carl Nägeli](/wiki/Carl_N%C3%A4geli "Carl Nägeli"). Silver ions damage the metabolism of bacteria even at such low concentrations as 0.01–0.1 milligrams per litre; metallic silver has a similar effect due to the formation of silver oxide. This effect is lost in the presence of [sulfur](/wiki/Sulfur "Sulfur") due to the extreme insolubility of silver sulfide.[134]

Some silver compounds are very explosive, such as the nitrogen compounds silver azide, silver [amide](/wiki/Amide "Amide"), and silver fulminate, as well as [silver acetylide](/wiki/Silver_acetylide "Silver acetylide"), [silver oxalate](/wiki/Silver_oxalate "Silver oxalate"), and silver(II) oxide. They can explode on heating, force, drying, illumination, or sometimes spontaneously. To avoid the formation of such compounds, ammonia and [acetylene](/wiki/Acetylene "Acetylene") should be kept away from silver equipment. Salts of silver with strongly oxidising acids such as [silver chlorate](/wiki/Silver_chlorate "Silver chlorate") and silver nitrate can explode on contact with materials that can be readily oxidised, such as organic compounds, sulfur and soot.[134]
