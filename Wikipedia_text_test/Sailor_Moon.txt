_**Sailor Moon**_ ([Japanese](/wiki/Japanese_language "Japanese language"): 美少女戦士セーラームーン, [Hepburn](/wiki/Hepburn_romanization "Hepburn romanization"): _Bishōjo Senshi Sērā Mūn_ ; originally translated as _**Pretty Soldier Sailor Moon**_ ,[1] later _**Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon**_[2][3]) is a Japanese [manga](/wiki/Manga "Manga") series written and illustrated by [Naoko Takeuchi](/wiki/Naoko_Takeuchi "Naoko Takeuchi"). It was originally serialized in [Kodansha](/wiki/Kodansha "Kodansha")'s [_shōjo_ manga](/wiki/Sh%C5%8Djo_manga "Shōjo manga") magazine _[Nakayoshi](/wiki/Nakayoshi "Nakayoshi")_ from 1991 to 1997; the 60 individual chapters (later reorganized into 52) and several side stories were compiled into 18 _[tankōbon](/wiki/Tank%C5%8Dbon "Tankōbon")_ volumes. Set in [Tokyo](/wiki/Tokyo "Tokyo") in the 1990s, the series follows the adventures of a schoolgirl named [Usagi Tsukino](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_\(character\) "Sailor Moon \(character\)") as she transforms into the eponymous character to search for a [magical artifact](/wiki/Magical_artifact "Magical artifact"), the "Legendary Silver Crystal" (「幻の銀水晶」, _Maboroshi no Ginsuishō_ ; lit. "Phantom Silver Crystal"). She leads a group of comrades, the Sailor Soldiers, called Sailor Guardians in later editions, as they battle against villains to prevent the theft of the Silver Crystal and the destruction of the [Solar System](/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System"). 

The manga was written in conjunction with the airing of [an anime series](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_\(TV_series\) "Sailor Moon \(TV series\)") produced by [Toei Animation](/wiki/Toei_Animation "Toei Animation"), which was broadcast in [Japan](/wiki/Japan "Japan") from 1992 to 1997.[4][5] Toei also developed three animated feature films, a television special, and three short films based on the anime. A live-action television adaptation, _[Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon](/wiki/Pretty_Guardian_Sailor_Moon_\(2003_TV_series\) "Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon \(2003 TV series\)")_ , aired from 2003 to 2004, and a second anime series, _[Sailor Moon Crystal](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_Crystal "Sailor Moon Crystal")_ , began [simulcasting](/wiki/Simulcast "Simulcast") in 2014. The manga series was licensed for an English release by [Kodansha Comics](/wiki/Kodansha_Comics "Kodansha Comics") in [North America](/wiki/North_America "North America"), and in [Australia](/wiki/Australia "Australia") and [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand "New Zealand") by Random House Australia. The entire anime series has been licensed by [Viz Media](/wiki/Viz_Media "Viz Media") for an English-language release in North America and by [Madman Entertainment](/wiki/Madman_Entertainment "Madman Entertainment") in Australia and New Zealand. 

Since its release, _Sailor Moon_ has received critical acclaim, with praise for its art, characterization, and humor. The manga has sold over 46 million copies worldwide, making it one of the [best-selling manga series](/wiki/List_of_best-selling_manga "List of best-selling manga"), as well as one of the best-selling [_shōjo_ manga](/wiki/Sh%C5%8Djo_manga "Shōjo manga") series of all time, marking its importance in the [history of manga](/wiki/History_of_manga "History of manga").[6] Additionally, the television series played a major role in popularizing anime in the [Western world](/wiki/Western_world "Western world"), particularly in the [United States](/wiki/United_States "United States"). The _Sailor Moon_ franchise has generated over $2.5 billion in worldwide merchandise sales. 

## Overview

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Overview")]

Further information: [List of _Sailor Moon_ characters](/wiki/List_of_Sailor_Moon_characters "List of Sailor Moon characters") and [List of _Sailor Moon_ chapters](/wiki/List_of_Sailor_Moon_chapters "List of Sailor Moon chapters")

The series focuses on [Usagi Tsukino](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_\(character\) "Sailor Moon \(character\)"), a young middle school student in the [Azabu-Jūban](/wiki/Azabu-J%C5%ABban "Azabu-Jūban") district of [Tokyo](/wiki/Tokyo "Tokyo") in the 1990s, who learns from the talking cat [Luna](/wiki/Luna_\(Sailor_Moon\) "Luna \(Sailor Moon\)") that she is actually a reincarnation of a princess from the Moon Kingdom, Sailor Moon. She is joined by Ami Mizuno, a student who awakens as [Sailor Mercury](/wiki/Sailor_Mercury "Sailor Mercury"); Rei Hino, a local [Shinto](/wiki/Shinto "Shinto") [shrine maiden](/wiki/Miko "Miko") who awakens as [Sailor Mars](/wiki/Sailor_Mars "Sailor Mars"); Makoto Kino, a tall and strong transfer student who awakens as [Sailor Jupiter](/wiki/Sailor_Jupiter "Sailor Jupiter"); and Minako Aino, a young aspiring [idol](/wiki/Japanese_idol "Japanese idol") who had awakened as [Sailor Venus](/wiki/Sailor_Venus "Sailor Venus") a few months earlier, accompanied by her talking feline companion [Artemis](/wiki/List_of_Sailor_Moon_characters#Artemis "List of Sailor Moon characters"). As they set out on their adventures, others join the Guardians later in the series, including Mamoru Chiba, a high school student who occasionally assists them as [Tuxedo Mask](/wiki/Tuxedo_Mask "Tuxedo Mask"); [Chibiusa](/wiki/Chibiusa "Chibiusa"), Usagi and Mamoru's future daughter who awakens as Sailor Chibi Moon; Haruka Tenoh, a car-racer who can transform into [Sailor Uranus](/wiki/Sailor_Uranus "Sailor Uranus"); Michiru Kaioh, a violinist who can transform into [Sailor Neptune](/wiki/Sailor_Neptune "Sailor Neptune"); Setsuna Meioh, a physics student who can transform into [Sailor Pluto](/wiki/Sailor_Pluto "Sailor Pluto"); Hotaru Tomoe, who awakens as [Sailor Saturn](/wiki/Sailor_Saturn "Sailor Saturn"); and the Three Lights, pop idols who are really the [Sailor Starlights](/wiki/Sailor_Starlights "Sailor Starlights"). Together, they encounter extraterrestrials, space travelers, and other Guardians as they protect the Earth and the Moon from destruction by malevolent forces.

### Concept and creation

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Concept and creation")]

[Naoko Takeuchi](/wiki/Naoko_Takeuchi "Naoko Takeuchi"), after working on the 1991 [Nami Akimoto](/wiki/Nami_Akimoto "Nami Akimoto") manga _[Miracle Girls](/wiki/Miracle_Girls "Miracle Girls")_ , redeveloped _Sailor Moon_ from her manga [one-shot](/wiki/One-shot_\(comics\) "One-shot \(comics\)") _[Codename: Sailor V](/wiki/Codename:_Sailor_V "Codename: Sailor V")_ , which was published on August 8, 1991, and featured Sailor Venus as the main protagonist.[7] Takeuchi wanted to create a story with a theme about girls in outer space. While discussing with her editor, Fumio Osano, he suggested the addition of [Sailor fuku](/wiki/Sailor_fuku "Sailor fuku").[8] When _Codename: Sailor V_ was proposed for adaptation into an anime by [Toei Animation](/wiki/Toei_Animation "Toei Animation"), Takeuchi redeveloped the concept so Sailor Venus became a member of a team.[9][10] The resulting manga series became a fusion of the popular magical girl genre and the _[Super Sentai](/wiki/Super_Sentai "Super Sentai")_ series, of which Takeuchi was a fan.[11] Recurring motifs include [astronomy](/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"),[8] [astrology](/wiki/Astrology "Astrology"), [gemology](/wiki/Gemology "Gemology"), [Greco-Roman mythology](/wiki/Classical_mythology "Classical mythology"),[12] [Japanese elemental themes](/wiki/Japanese_mythology "Japanese mythology"),[13]: 286 teenage fashion trends,[12][14] and schoolgirl antics.[14] _Codename: Sailor V_ would continue to run parallel to _Sailor Moon_ as a serial, now recontextualized as an [origin story](/wiki/Origin_story "Origin story") for Sailor Venus. 

Takeuchi said discussions with Kodansha originally envisaged a single story arc;[15] the storyline was developed in meetings a year before serialization began.[16]: 93 After completing the arc, Toei and Kodansha asked Takeuchi to continue the series. She wrote four more story arcs,[15] which were often published simultaneously with the five corresponding seasons of the anime adaptation. The anime ran one or two months behind the manga.[16]: 93 As a result, the anime follows the storyline of the manga fairly closely, although there are deviations.[17] Takeuchi later said because Toei's production staff was mostly male, she felt the anime had "a slight male perspective".[17]

Takeuchi later said that she planned to kill off the protagonists, but Osano rejected the notion and said, "[_Sailor Moon_] is a [_shōjo_ manga](/wiki/Sh%C5%8Djo_manga "Shōjo manga")!" When the anime adaptation was produced, the protagonists were killed in the final battle with the Dark Kingdom, although they were revived. Takeuchi resented that she was unable to do that in her version.[18] Takeuchi also intended for the _Sailor Moon_ anime adaptation to last for one season, but due to its immense popularity, Toei asked Takeuchi to continue the series. At first, she struggled to develop another storyline to extend the series. While discussing with Osano, he suggested the inclusion of Usagi's daughter from the future, Chibiusa.[18]

### Westernization

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Westernization")]

When the _Sailor Moon_ anime was first released in North America and dubbed in English in 1995, fans and academics alike noted that the dub had westernized the series by altering or removing Japanese names and cultural references.[19] These included, but were not limited to, Sailor Moon's real name, Usagi Tsukino, being changed to Serena,[19] and the name of Mamoru Chiba, Usagi's love interest, being changed to Darien Shields.[20] The original main theme was changed from a romance ballad discussing Serena's (Usagi's) eternal love for Darien (Mamoru) to an anthem focused on Serena's newfound identity as the superheroine Sailor Moon.[21] The nature of Serena and Darien's relationship – a minor who is romantically involved with an adult – was interestingly unchanged from the Japanese anime, although attempts were made to obscure the age difference in the Western adaptations.[22]

Other examples of westernization referenced by _Sailor Moon_ 's audience included flipping scenes of traffic to have cars [drive on the right side of the road](/wiki/Left-_and_right-hand_traffic "Left- and right-hand traffic"), along with the English dub changing any conversations between characters that contained lesser-known Japanese cultural references,[23] removing violent scenes, reducing visible nudity. The North American, Italian, and Latin American adaptations altered LGBT+ characters, such as depicting the lesbian couple of Amara (Haruka) and Michelle (Michiru) as cousins, creating problematic subtexts that do not exist in the original work, changing the gay couple of Zoisite and Malachite (Kunzite) into a heterosexual couple by making Zoisite a woman, and converting the antagonist Fisheye from biological male with female gender representation who shows sexual interest in males to a female character.[23]

According to [Bandai America](/wiki/Bandai_America "Bandai America"), the company in charge of Sailor Moon merchandise in the [Western Hemisphere](/wiki/Western_Hemisphere "Western Hemisphere"), the approach to advertising _Sailor Moon_ was to make the show and superheroine "'culturally appropriate' for the American market".[24]

### Manga

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Manga")]

Main article: [List of _Sailor Moon_ chapters](/wiki/List_of_Sailor_Moon_chapters "List of Sailor Moon chapters")

Written and illustrated by [Naoko Takeuchi](/wiki/Naoko_Takeuchi "Naoko Takeuchi"), _Sailor Moon_ was serialized by [Kodansha](/wiki/Kodansha "Kodansha") in the [_shōjo_ manga](/wiki/Sh%C5%8Djo_manga "Shōjo manga") anthology _[Nakayoshi](/wiki/Nakayoshi "Nakayoshi")_ from December 28, 1991, to February 3, 1997.[7] The side-stories were serialized simultaneously in _RunRun_ —another of Kodansha's manga magazines.[7] The 52 individual chapters were published in 18 _[tankōbon](/wiki/Tank%C5%8Dbon "Tankōbon")_ volumes by Kodansha from July 6, 1992, to April 4, 1997.[25][26] In 2003, the chapters were re-released in a collection of 12 _shinzōban_ volumes to coincide with the release of the [live-action series](/wiki/Pretty_Guardian_Sailor_Moon_\(live-action_series\) "Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon \(live-action series\)").[27] The manga was retitled _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon_ and included new cover art,[28] and revised dialogue and illustrations. The ten individual short stories were also released in two volumes.[29][30] In 2013, the chapters were once again re-released in 10 _[kanzenban](/wiki/Kanzenban "Kanzenban")_ volumes to commemorate the manga's 20th anniversary, which includes digitally remastered artwork, new covers, and color artwork from its _Nakayoshi_ run.[31] The books were enlarged from the typical Japanese manga size to A5.[32][33] The short stories were republished in two volumes, with the order of the stories shuffled. _Codename: Sailor V_ was also included in the third edition.[33]

The _Sailor Moon_ manga was initially licensed for an English release by Mixx (later [Tokyopop](/wiki/Tokyopop "Tokyopop")) in North America. The manga was first published as a serial in _[MixxZine](/wiki/TOKYOPOP_\(magazine\) "TOKYOPOP \(magazine\)")_ beginning in 1997, but was later removed from the magazine and made into a separate, low print monthly comic to finish the first, second, and third arcs. At the same time, the fourth and fifth arcs were printed in a secondary magazine, _[Smile](/wiki/Smile_\(magazine\) "Smile \(magazine\)")_.[34] Pages from the Tokyopop version of the manga ran daily in the Japanimation Station, a service accessible to users of [America Online](/wiki/America_Online "America Online").[35] The series was later collected into a three-part graphic novel series spanning eighteen volumes, which were published from December 1, 1998, to September 18, 2001.[36][37] In May 2005, Tokyopop's license to the _Sailor Moon_ manga expired, and its edition went out of print.[38]

In 2011, [Kodansha Comics](/wiki/Kodansha_Comics "Kodansha Comics") announced that the company had acquired the license for the _Sailor Moon_ manga and its lead-in series, _Codename: Sailor V,_ in English.[39] They published the twelve volumes of _Sailor Moon_ simultaneously with the two-volume edition of _Codename: Sailor V_ from September 2011 to July 2013.[40][41][42] The first of the two related short story volumes was published on September 10, 2013;[43] the second was published on November 26, 2013.[44] At [Anime Expo](/wiki/Anime_Expo "Anime Expo") 2017, Kodansha Comics announced plans to re-release _Sailor Moon_ in an "Eternal Edition", featuring a new English translation, new cover artwork by Takeuchi, and color pages from the manga's original run, printed on extra-large premium paper.[45][46] The first Eternal Edition volume was published on September 11, 2018;[47] the tenth and final volume was published on October 20, 2020.[48] On July 1, 2019, Kondasha Comics began releasing the Eternal Editions digitally,[49] following an announcement the day before about the series being released digitally in ten different languages.[50] In November 2020, Kodansha Comics announced plans to re-release the _Sailor Moon_ manga again as part of their "Naoko Takeuchi Collection".[51] The company described the new edition as a "more affordable, portable" version of the Eternal Edition. The first volume was published on April 5, 2022,[52] and the last on September 17, 2024. 

_Sailor Moon_ has also been licensed in other English-speaking countries. In the United Kingdom, the volumes are distributed by Turnaround Publisher Services.[53] In Australia, the manga is distributed by Penguin Books Australia.[54]

The manga has been licensed in Russia as well as the [CIS](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Independent_States "Commonwealth of Independent States") for distribution by publishing company XL Media. The first volume was released in 2018.[55]

#### _Sailor Moon_

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Sailor Moon")]

Main article: [_Sailor Moon_ (TV series)](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_\(TV_series\) "Sailor Moon \(TV series\)")

Toei Animation produced an anime television series based on the 52 manga chapters, also titled _Pretty Soldier Sailor Moon_.[4][5] [Junichi Sato](/wiki/Junichi_Sato "Junichi Sato") directed the first season, [Kunihiko Ikuhara](/wiki/Kunihiko_Ikuhara "Kunihiko Ikuhara") took over the second through fourth season, and [Takuya Igarashi](/wiki/Takuya_Igarashi "Takuya Igarashi") directed the fifth and final season.[56] The series premiered in Japan on [TV Asahi](/wiki/TV_Asahi "TV Asahi") on March 7, 1992, and ran for 200 episodes until its conclusion on February 8, 1997. Upon its release, the show quickly rose to be Toei Animation's highest-rated TV series.[57] Most of the international versions, including the English adaptations, are titled _Sailor Moon_. 

#### _Sailor Moon Crystal_

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Sailor Moon Crystal")]

Main article: [_Sailor Moon Crystal_](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_Crystal "Sailor Moon Crystal")

On July 6, 2012, Kodansha and Toei Animation announced that it would commence the production of a new anime adaptation of _Sailor Moon_ , called _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon Crystal_ , for a simultaneous worldwide release in 2013 as part of the series's 20th anniversary celebrations,[58][59][60] and stated that it would be a closer adaptation of the manga than the first anime.[61] _Crystal_ premiered on July 5, 2014, and new episodes would air on the first and third Saturdays of each month.[62] A new cast was announced, along with [Kotono Mitsuishi](/wiki/Kotono_Mitsuishi "Kotono Mitsuishi") reprising her role as Sailor Moon.[63] The first two seasons were released together, covering their corresponding arcs of the manga (_Dark Kingdom_ and _Black Moon_). A third season based on the _Infinity_ arc of the manga premiered on Japanese television on April 4, 2016, known as _Death Busters_ in this adaptation.[64] [Munehisa Sakai](/wiki/Munehisa_Sakai "Munehisa Sakai") directed the first and second seasons, while [Chiaki Kon](/wiki/Chiaki_Kon "Chiaki Kon") directed the third season. 

#### Films & television specials

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Films & television specials")]

Three animated theatrical feature films based on the original _Sailor Moon_ series have been released in Japan: _[Sailor Moon R: The Movie](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_R:_The_Movie "Sailor Moon R: The Movie")_ in 1993, followed by _[Sailor Moon S: The Movie](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_S:_The_Movie "Sailor Moon S: The Movie")_ in 1994, and _[Sailor Moon SuperS: The Movie](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_SuperS:_The_Movie "Sailor Moon SuperS: The Movie")_ in 1995. The films are side-stories that do not correlate with the timeline of the original series. A one-hour television special was aired on TV Asahi in Japan on April 8, 1995.[65] [Kunihiko Ikuhara](/wiki/Kunihiko_Ikuhara "Kunihiko Ikuhara") directed the first film, while the latter two were directed by Hiroki Shibata. 

In 1997, an article in _[Variety](/wiki/Variety_\(magazine\) "Variety \(magazine\)")_ stated that [The Walt Disney Company](/wiki/The_Walt_Disney_Company "The Walt Disney Company") was interested in acquiring the rights to _Sailor Moon_ as a live action film to be directed by [Stanley Tong](/wiki/Stanley_Tong "Stanley Tong") and [Geena Davis](/wiki/Geena_Davis "Geena Davis") set to portray as Queen Beryl, along with [Winona Ryder](/wiki/Winona_Ryder "Winona Ryder") and [Elisabeth Shue](/wiki/Elisabeth_Shue "Elisabeth Shue") starring as well.[66] After Disney put the project on [turnaround](/wiki/Turnaround_\(filmmaking\) "Turnaround \(filmmaking\)"), [Universal Pictures](/wiki/Universal_Pictures "Universal Pictures") acquired the film rights.[67]

In 2017, it was revealed that _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon Crystal_ anime's fourth season would be produced as a two-part theatrical anime film project, adapting the _Dream_ arc from the manga.[68] On June 30, 2019, it was announced that the title of the film would be _[Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon Eternal The Movie](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_Eternal "Sailor Moon Eternal")_.[69][70] The first film was to be released on September 11, 2020,[71] but was postponed and released on January 8, 2021, and the second film was released on February 11, 2021.[72] [Chiaki Kon](/wiki/Chiaki_Kon "Chiaki Kon") returned from _Crystal_ 's third season to direct the two films.[68]

In 2022, it was announced that a sequel to _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon Eternal The Movie_ , covering the _Stars_ arc of the manga, would also be produced as a two-part theatrical anime film project, titled _[Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon Cosmos The Movie](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_Cosmos "Sailor Moon Cosmos")_. The two films were directed by Tomoya Takahashi and were released on June 9 and 30, 2023, respectively.[73][74]

### Companion books

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Companion books")]

There have been numerous companion books to _Sailor Moon_. Kodansha released some of these books for each of the five story arcs, collectively called the _Original Picture Collection_. The books contain cover art, promotional material, and other work by Takeuchi. Many of the drawings are accompanied by comments on the way she developed her ideas, created each picture, and commentary on the anime adaption of her story.[1][75][76][77][78] Another picture collection, _Volume Infinity_ , was released as a self-published, limited-edition art book after the end of the series in 1997. This art book includes drawings by Takeuchi and her friends, her staff, and many of the voice actors who worked on the anime. In 1999, Kodansha published the _Materials Collection_ ; this contained development sketches and notes for nearly every character in the manga, and for some characters that never appeared. Each drawing includes notes by Takeuchi about costume pieces, the mentality of the characters, and her feelings about them. It also includes timelines for the story arcs and for the real-life release of products and materials relating to the anime and manga. A short story, _Parallel Sailor Moon,_ is also featured, celebrating the [year of the rabbit](/wiki/Rabbit_\(zodiac\) "Rabbit \(zodiac\)").[15]

### Novels

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Novels")]

_Sailor Moon_ was also adapted for publication as novels and released in 1998. The first book was written by [Stuart J. Levy](/wiki/Stuart_J._Levy "Stuart J. Levy"). The following novels were written by Lianne Sentar.[79] From 2018–2019, the [novelization](/wiki/Novelization "Novelization") of the first story arc of the manga was adapted by Miyoko Ikeda into three volumes.[80][81][82][83]

### Stage musicals

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Stage musicals")]

Main article: [_Sailor Moon_ musicals](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_musicals "Sailor Moon musicals")

In mid-1993, the first musical theater production based on _Sailor Moon_ premiered and starred [Anza Ohyama](/wiki/Anza_Ohyama "Anza Ohyama") as Sailor Moon. Thirty such musicals in all have been produced, with one in pre-production. The show’s stories include anime-inspired plotlines and original material. Music from the series has been released on about 20 memorial albums.[84] The popularity of the musicals has been cited as a reason behind the production of the live-action television series, _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon_.[85]

During the original, run musicals ran in the winter and summer of each year, with summer musicals staged at the [Sunshine](/wiki/Sunshine_City,_Tokyo "Sunshine City, Tokyo") Theater in the [Ikebukuro](/wiki/Ikebukuro "Ikebukuro") area of Tokyo. In the winter, musicals toured to other large cities in Japan, including [Osaka](/wiki/Osaka "Osaka"), [Fukuoka](/wiki/Fukuoka "Fukuoka"),[86] [Nagoya](/wiki/Nagoya "Nagoya"), [Shizuoka](/wiki/Shizuoka,_Shizuoka "Shizuoka, Shizuoka"), [Kanazawa](/wiki/Kanazawa,_Ishikawa "Kanazawa, Ishikawa"), [Sendai](/wiki/Sendai "Sendai"),[87] [Saga](/wiki/Saga,_Saga "Saga, Saga"), [Oita](/wiki/%C5%8Cita,_%C5%8Cita "Ōita, Ōita"), [Yamagata](/wiki/Yamagata,_Yamagata "Yamagata, Yamagata"), and [Fukushima](/wiki/Fukushima,_Fukushima "Fukushima, Fukushima").[88] The final incarnation of the first run, _New Legend of Kaguya Island (Revised Edition)_ (新・かぐや島伝説 <改訂版>, _Shin Kaguyashima Densetsu (Kaiteban)_), went on stage in January 2005, following which, [Bandai](/wiki/Bandai "Bandai") officially put the series on a hiatus.[89] On June 2, 2013, Fumio Osano announced on his Twitter page that the _Sailor Moon_ musicals would begin again in September 2013.[90] The 20th anniversary show _La Reconquista_ ran from September 13 to 23 at Shibuya's AiiA Theater Tokyo, with Satomi Ōkubo as Sailor Moon. Satomi Ōkubo reprised the role in the 2014 production _Petite Étrangère,_ which ran from August 21 to September 7, 2014, again at AiiA Theater Tokyo.

#### Unrealized American adaptation

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Unrealized American adaptation")]

Main article: [_Sailor Moon_ (1994 TV pilot)](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_\(1994_TV_pilot\) "Sailor Moon \(1994 TV pilot\)")

In 1993, Renaissance-Atlantic Entertainment, Bandai, and Toon Makers, Inc. conceptualized their own version of _Sailor Moon_ , which was half live-action and half Western-style animation.[98][99] Toon Makers produced a 17-minute [proof of concept](/wiki/Proof_of_concept "Proof of concept") pilot and a two-minute music video, both of which were directed by Rocky Solotoff, who also worked on the pilot's script.[100] Renaissance-Atlantic presented the concept to Toei, but it was turned down, as their concept would have cost significantly more than simply exporting and dubbing the anime adaptation.[101] The companies' work is believed by Solotoff to have been handed over to Raymond Iacovacci, one of the producers on the project, who stored the pilot script and animation cels in a storage facility.[102] The logo created for the pilot was kept for the English dub, and Bandai released a "Moon Cycle" as part of its merchandise for the show, based on vehicles designed for the pilot.[101]

The project was rediscovered in 1998 when the music video was screened at the [Anime Expo](/wiki/Anime_Expo "Anime Expo") convention in Los Angeles,[103] where it was met with laughter by onlookers.[104][105] A congoer recorded the music video and the audience response, which would later resurface on video sites such as [YouTube](/wiki/YouTube "YouTube").[106] The pilot and the music video would go on to be discussed at conventions such as the 2011 [Gen Con](/wiki/Gen_Con "Gen Con") and 2012 [Anime Expo](/wiki/Anime_Expo "Anime Expo").[107][108] It was given the monikers of "_Toon Makers' Sailor Moon"_ and "Saban Moon", despite having no connection with [Saban Entertainment](/wiki/Saban_Entertainment "Saban Entertainment") save for Renaissance-Atlantic Entertainment, which worked with the company on _[Power Rangers](/wiki/Power_Rangers "Power Rangers")_.[109][110] The proof of concept video was widely presumed to be [lost](/wiki/Lost_media "Lost media"), and director Solotoff reported that he was frequently contacted by people searching for the pilot.[102][111] However, in 2012, multiple animation cels from the pilot, along with the script, surfaced on the internet after a storage locker, believed to be the one owned by Iacovacci, were sold.[102]

In 1998, Frank Ward, along with his company Renaissance-Atlantic Entertainment, tried to revive the idea of doing a live-action series based on Sailor Moon, this time called _Team Angel_ , without the involvement of Toon Makers. A 2-minute reel was produced and sent to Bandai America, but was also rejected.[102]

In August 2022, the proof of concept was showcased for the first time on [YouTube](/wiki/YouTube "YouTube") in a documentary by Ray Mona. Mona obtained both the pilot and its music video, as well as its related materials, from the [Library of Congress](/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress") in [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C. "Washington, D.C.")[112]

#### _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon_

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon")]

Main article: [_Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon_ (2003 TV series)](/wiki/Pretty_Guardian_Sailor_Moon_\(2003_TV_series\) "Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon \(2003 TV series\)")

In 2003, [Toei Company](/wiki/Toei_Company "Toei Company") produced a Japanese live action _Sailor Moon_ television series using the new translated English title of _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon_. Its 49 episodes were broadcast on [Chubu-Nippon Broadcasting](/wiki/Chubu-Nippon_Broadcasting "Chubu-Nippon Broadcasting") from October 4, 2003, to September 25, 2004.[113][114] _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon_ featured [Miyuu Sawai](/wiki/Miyuu_Sawai "Miyuu Sawai") as Usagi Tsukino, [Rika Izumi](/wiki/Rika_Izumi "Rika Izumi") (credited as Chisaki Hama) as Ami Mizuno, [Keiko Kitagawa](/wiki/Keiko_Kitagawa "Keiko Kitagawa") as Rei Hino, [Mew Azama](/wiki/Mew_Azama "Mew Azama") as Makoto Kino, [Ayaka Komatsu](/wiki/Ayaka_Komatsu "Ayaka Komatsu") as Minako Aino, [Jouji Shibue](/wiki/Jouji_Shibue "Jouji Shibue") as Mamoru Chiba, [Keiko Han](/wiki/Keiko_Han "Keiko Han") reprising her voice role as Luna from the original anime, and [Kappei Yamaguchi](/wiki/Kappei_Yamaguchi "Kappei Yamaguchi") voicing Artemis. The series was an alternate retelling of the Dark Kingdom arc, adding a storyline different from that in the manga and first anime series, with original characters, and new plot developments.[85][115] In addition to the main episodes, two [direct-to-video](/wiki/Direct-to-video "Direct-to-video") releases appeared after the show ended its television broadcast. "Special Act" is set four years after the main storyline ends, and shows the wedding of the two main characters. "Act Zero" is a prequel showing the origins of [Sailor V](/wiki/Codename:_Sailor_V "Codename: Sailor V") and Tuxedo Mask.[116]

### Video games

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Video games")]

See also: [List of _Sailor Moon_ video games](/wiki/List_of_Sailor_Moon_video_games "List of Sailor Moon video games")

The _Sailor Moon_ franchise has spawned several video games across various genres and platforms. Most were made by Bandai and its subsidiary Angel; others were produced by [Banpresto](/wiki/Banpresto "Banpresto"). The early games were [side-scrolling fighters](/wiki/Beat_%27em_up "Beat 'em up"); later ones were unique [puzzle games](/wiki/Computer_puzzle_game "Computer puzzle game") or [versus fighting games](/wiki/Versus_fighting_game "Versus fighting game"). _[Another Story](/wiki/Sailor_Moon:_Another_Story "Sailor Moon: Another Story")_ was a turn-based [role-playing video game](/wiki/Role-playing_video_game "Role-playing video game").[117] The only _Sailor Moon_ game produced outside Japan, 3VR New Media's _The 3D Adventures of Sailor Moon_ , went on sale in North America in 1997. They were developed in association with [DIC Entertainment](/wiki/DIC_Entertainment "DIC Entertainment"), which held the rights to the game and the TV series.[118] A video game called _Sailor Moon: La Luna Splende_ (_Sailor Moon: The Moon Shines_) was released on March 16, 2011, for the [Nintendo DS](/wiki/Nintendo_DS "Nintendo DS").[119] A mobile game, titled _[Sailor Moon Drops](/wiki/Sailor_Moon_Drops "Sailor Moon Drops")_ , was available in Japan on September 3, 2015, and released world-wide on April 12, 2016, for iOS and Android. The mobile game was ultimately shut down on March 28, 2019.[120]

The Dyskami Publishing Company released _Sailor Moon Crystal Dice Challenge_ , created by [James Ernest](/wiki/James_Ernest "James Ernest") of Cheapass Games and based on the _[Button Men](/wiki/Button_Men "Button Men")_ tabletop game, in 2017, and _Sailor Moon Crystal Truth or Bluff_ in 2018.[121][122][123]

### Theme park attractions

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Theme park attractions")]

A Sailor Moon attraction, _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon: The Miracle 4-D_ , was announced for [Universal Studios Japan](/wiki/Universal_Studios_Japan "Universal Studios Japan").[124] It featured Sailor Moon and the Inner Guardians arriving at the theme park, only to discover and stop the Youma's plan from stealing people's energies. The attraction ran from March 16 through July 24, 2018. 

The sequel attraction, _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon The Miracle 4-D: Moon Palace Chapter_ , ran from May 31, 2019, to August 25, 2019. It featured all 10 Sailor Guardians and Super Sailor Moon.[125]

In January 2022, a new attraction was announced titled _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon The Miracle 4-D: Moon Palace Chapter Deluxe_. The attraction features the same storyline as the last and features the Sailor Guardians in their princess forms. It ran from March 4, 2022, to August 28, 2022.[126] The same attraction is expected to release in [Universal Studios Hollywood](/wiki/Universal_Studios_Hollywood "Universal Studios Hollywood") in April 2026 as part of [Universal Fan Fest Nights](/wiki/Universal_Fan_Fest_Nights "Universal Fan Fest Nights").[127]

### Ice skating show

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Ice skating show")]

An [ice skating show](/wiki/Ice_show "Ice show") of _Sailor Moon_ was announced on June 30, 2019, starring [Evgenia Medvedeva](/wiki/Evgenia_Medvedeva "Evgenia Medvedeva") as the lead.[128] The name for the ice-skating show was announced as _Pretty Guardian Sailor Moon: Prism on Ice_ , as well as the additional casts, with [Anza](/wiki/Anza_\(singer\) "Anza \(singer\)") from the first _Sailor Moon_ musicals to play Queen Serenity, and the main voice actresses of the _Sailor Moon Crystal_ anime series to voice their individual characters. Takuya Hiramatsu from the musicals was to write the screenplay, Yuka Sato and Benji Schwimmer were to be in charge of choreography, and Akiko Kosaka & Gesshoku Kaigi were to write the music for the show.[129] The show was set to debut in early June 2020, but was first postponed to June 2021, and later to June 2022, due to the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic"),[130][131][132] and finally cancelled on February 23, 2023, due to an "unstable world situation", following the [Russo-Ukrainian war](/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war "Russo-Ukrainian war").[133]

### Idol group

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Idol group")]

Main article: [F5ve](/wiki/F5ve "F5ve")

A Japanese idol pop group named [SG5](/wiki/F5ve "F5ve"), short for Sailor Guardians 5, was announced in June 2022. Early plans to form the group began in 2020, with the official lineup and overall concept finalized in 2022. As part of the process, the group had to seek the approval of Naoko Takeuchi by performing in front of her and giving a presentation. The group consists of five members: Sayaka, Ruri, Miyuu, [Kaede](/wiki/Kaede_\(dancer\) "Kaede \(dancer\)"), and Rui. They officially debuted in July 2022 at Anime Expo.[134][135][136] On March 1, 2023, SG5 released their debut single "Firetruck", alongside a music video with references to the manga.[137] The group re-debuted under the name F5ve with the single "Lettuce" in May 2024, ceasing their association with _Sailor Moon_.[138]

## Reception

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Reception")]

_Sailor Moon_ is one of the most popular manga series of all time and continues to enjoy high readership worldwide. More than one million copies of its _tankōbon_ volumes had been sold in Japan by the end of 1995.[16]: 95 The series and its characters have been described as iconic.[139] By the series's 20th anniversary in 2012, the manga had sold over 35 million copies in over fifty countries,[140] and the franchise has generated $2.5 billion in worldwide [merchandise](/wiki/Merchandise "Merchandise") sales as of 1996.[141] By 1995, Sailor Moon merchandise was bringing in more than $250 million per year in Japan.[142] The manga won the [Kodansha Manga Award](/wiki/Kodansha_Manga_Award "Kodansha Manga Award") in 1993 in the _shōjo_ genre.[143] The English adaptations of both the manga and the anime series became the first successful _shōjo_ title in the United States.[144] The character of Sailor Moon is recognized as one of the most popular and influential [female superheroes](/wiki/Female_superheroes "Female superheroes") of all time.[145][146][147][148]

The 1992 _Sailor Moon_ anime was broadcast in Spain and France beginning in December 1993; these became the first countries outside Japan to broadcast the series.[149] It was later aired in Russia, South Korea, the Philippines, China, Italy, Taiwan, Thailand, Indonesia, and Hong Kong, before North America picked up the franchise for adaptation. In the Philippines, _Sailor Moon_ was one of [its carrier network](/wiki/TV5_Network "TV5 Network")'s main draws, helping it to become the third-biggest network in the country.[13]: 10–11 In 2001, the _Sailor Moon_ manga was Tokyopop's best-selling property, outselling the next-best-selling titles by at least a factor of 1.5.[150] In [Diamond Comic Distributors](/wiki/Diamond_Comic_Distributors "Diamond Comic Distributors")’ May 1999 "Graphic Novel and Trade Paperback" category, _Sailor Moon_ Volume 3 was the best-selling comic book in the United States.[151]

Academic Timothy J. Craig attributes _Sailor Moon_ 's international success to three characteristics: the show's magical girl transformation of ordinary characters into superheroes, the ability of marketers to establish the international audience's connection to characters, despite their culture being Japanese, and a female superhero protagonist, something which was still rare in global pop culture during the 1990s.[152]

In his 2007 book _[Manga: The Complete Guide](/wiki/Manga:_The_Complete_Guide "Manga: The Complete Guide")_ , [Jason Thompson](/wiki/Jason_Thompson_\(writer\) "Jason Thompson \(writer\)") gave the manga series three stars out of four. He enjoyed the blending of _[shōnen](/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dnen_manga "Shōnen manga")_ and _shōjo_ styles and said the combat scenes seemed heavily influenced by _[Saint Seiya](/wiki/Saint_Seiya "Saint Seiya")_ , but shorter and less bloody. He also said the manga itself appeared similar to _Super Sentai_ television shows. Thompson found the series fun and entertaining, but said the repetitive plot lines were a detriment to the title, which the increasing quality of art could not make up for; even so, he called the series "sweet, effective entertainment".[144] Thompson said that although the audience for _Sailor Moon_ is both female and male, Takeuchi does not use [fan service](/wiki/Fanservice "Fanservice") for males, which would run the risk of alienating her female audience. Thompson said fight scenes are not physical and "boil down to their purest form of a clash of wills", which he says "makes thematic sense" for the manga.[153] [Venita Blackburn](/wiki/Venita_Blackburn "Venita Blackburn") of _[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_ opined that _Sailor Moon_ is a "transformative experience" about friendship and liberation that does not match the world's expectations of femininity, adding that its "iconic status" within the [queer community](/wiki/Queer_community "Queer community") was "no accident". She stated that the world of _Sailor Moon_ is "interested in transformation, in upsetting expectations of presentation and value related to girlhood, masculinity, strength and gender roles", further adding that in _Sailor Moon,_ the concept of transformation is "about light, magic and power hidden in the ordinariness of living", concluding that "there is nothing queerer than that".[154]

While comparing the manga and anime, Sylvain Durand thought that the manga artwork was"gorgeous", but that its storytelling was more compressed and erratic, and the anime had more character development. Durand said "the sense of tragedy is greater" in the manga's telling of the "fall of the Silver Millennium," giving more detail about the origins of the Four Kings of Heaven and in Usagi's final battle against Queen Beryl and Metaria. Durand said the anime omits information that makes the story easy to understand, but judges the anime as more "coherent" with a better balance of comedy and tragedy, whereas the manga is "more tragic" and focused on Usagi and Mamoru's romance.[155]

For the week of September 11, 2011, to September 17, 2011, the first volume of the re-released _Sailor Moon_ manga was the best-selling manga on [_The New York Times_ Manga Best Sellers list](/wiki/List_of_The_New_York_Times_Manga_Best_Sellers "List of The New York Times Manga Best Sellers"), with the first volume of _Codename: Sailor V_ in second place.[156][157] The first print run of the first volume sold out after four weeks.[158]

In English-speaking countries, _Sailor Moon_ developed a cult following among anime fans and university students.[12] Patrick Drazen says the [Internet](/wiki/History_of_the_World_Wide_Web "History of the World Wide Web") was a new medium that fans used to communicate and played a role in the popularity of _Sailor Moon_.[13]: 281 Fans could use the Internet to discuss the series, organize campaigns to return _Sailor Moon_ to U.S. broadcast, share information about episodes that had not yet aired, or write [fan fiction](/wiki/Fan_fiction "Fan fiction").[159][160] Gemma Cox of [_Neo_ magazine](/wiki/Neo_\(magazine\) "Neo \(magazine\)") said part of the series's allure was that fans communicated via the Internet about the differences between the dub and the original version.[161]

## Cultural impact and legacy

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Cultural impact and legacy")]

[](/wiki/File:Sailor_Moon_cosplayer_at_FanimeCon_2010-05-30_3.JPG)A _Sailor Moon_ cosplayer

With their dynamic heroines and action-oriented plots, _Sailor Moon_ is widely credited with reinvigorating the magical girl genre. After its success, many similar magical girl series, including _[Magic Knight Rayearth](/wiki/Magic_Knight_Rayearth "Magic Knight Rayearth")_ , _[Wedding Peach](/wiki/Wedding_Peach "Wedding Peach")_ , _[Saint Tail](/wiki/Saint_Tail "Saint Tail")_ , _[Nurse Angel Ririka SOS](/wiki/Nurse_Angel_Ririka_SOS "Nurse Angel Ririka SOS")_ , _[Cyber Team in Akihabara](/wiki/Cyber_Team_in_Akihabara "Cyber Team in Akihabara")_ , _[Corrector Yui](/wiki/Corrector_Yui "Corrector Yui"),_ and _[Pretty Cure](/wiki/Pretty_Cure "Pretty Cure")_ , emerged.[144]: 199 [162] _Sailor Moon_ has been called "the biggest breakthrough" in English-dubbed anime until 1995, when it premiered on [YTV](/wiki/YTV_\(Canadian_TV_channel\) "YTV \(Canadian TV channel\)"),[13]: 10–11 and "the pinnacle of little kid _shōjo_ anime".[163] Cultural anthropologist [Rachel Thorn](/wiki/Rachel_Thorn "Rachel Thorn") said that soon after _Sailor Moon_ , _shōjo_ manga started appearing in bookshops instead of fandom-dominated comic shops.[164] The series is seen as kickstarting a wider movement of girls taking up _shōjo_ manga.[144][165] Canadian librarian [Gilles Poitras](/wiki/Gilles_Poitras "Gilles Poitras") defines a generation of anime fans as those who were introduced to anime by _Sailor Moon_ in the 1990s, saying they were both much younger than other fans and were also mostly female.[162]

Historian [Fred Patten](/wiki/Fred_Patten "Fred Patten") credits Takeuchi with popularizing the concept of a _Super Sentai_ -like team of magical girls,[166][167] and Paul Gravett credits the series with revitalizing the magical girl genre itself.[168] A reviewer for _THEM Anime Reviews_ also credited the anime series with changing the genre—its heroine must use her powers to fight evil, not simply have fun as previous magical girls had done.[169] The series has been compared to _[Mighty Morphin Power Rangers](/wiki/Mighty_Morphin_Power_Rangers "Mighty Morphin Power Rangers")_ ,[12][170] _[Buffy the Vampire Slayer](/wiki/Buffy_the_Vampire_Slayer "Buffy the Vampire Slayer")_ ,[13]: 281 [171][172] and _[Sabrina the Teenage Witch](/wiki/Sabrina_the_Teenage_Witch_\(1996_TV_series\) "Sabrina the Teenage Witch \(1996 TV series\)")_.[173] Sailor Moon also influenced the development of _[Miraculous: Tales of Ladybug& Cat Noir](/wiki/Miraculous:_Tales_of_Ladybug_%26_Cat_Noir "Miraculous: Tales of Ladybug & Cat Noir")_, _[W.I.T.C.H.](/wiki/W.I.T.C.H. "W.I.T.C.H.")_ , _[Winx Club](/wiki/Winx_Club "Winx Club")_ , _[LoliRock](/wiki/LoliRock "LoliRock")_ , _[Star vs. the Forces of Evil](/wiki/Star_vs._the_Forces_of_Evil "Star vs. the Forces of Evil")_ , _[Robot Chicken](/wiki/Robot_Chicken "Robot Chicken")_ , _[Steven Universe](/wiki/Steven_Universe "Steven Universe")_ , and _[Totally Spies!](/wiki/Totally_Spies! "Totally Spies!")_.[174]

_Sailor Moon_ is associated with the [feminist](/wiki/Feminist "Feminist") and [girl power](/wiki/Girl_power "Girl power") movements and with empowering its female viewers,[175] especially regarding the "credible, charismatic and independent" characterizations of the Sailor Guardians.[176] _Sailor Moon_ is regarded as empowering to women and feminism in concept, particularly through the aggressive nature and strong personalities of the Guardians.[177] It represents a specific type of feminist concept where "traditional feminine ideals [are] incorporated into characters that act in traditionally male capacities".[177] While the Sailor Guardians are strong, independent fighters who thwart evil—which is generally a masculine stereotype—they are also ideally feminized in their Guardian transformation.[12]

The most notable hyper-feminine features of the Sailor Guardians—and most other females in Japanese girls' comics—are the girls' thin bodies, long legs, and, in particular, round, orb-like eyes.[12] Eyes are commonly known as the primal source within characters where emotion is evoked—sensitive characters have larger eyes than more stoic ones.[177] The stereotypical role of women in Japanese culture is to undertake romantic and loving feelings;[12] therefore, the prevalence of hyper-feminine qualities like the openness of the female eye in Japanese girls' comics is clearly exhibited in _Sailor Moon_. Thus, _Sailor Moon_ emphasizes a type of feminist model by combining traditionally masculine action with traditionally feminine affection through the Sailor Guardians.[177]

## Merchandise

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sailor_Moon&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Merchandise")]

Since the early 2000s, [Toei Animation](/wiki/Toei_Animation "Toei Animation") has collaborated with various different brands to create merchandise outside of a children's demographic.[178] On February 20, 2020, [ColourPop](/wiki/ColourPop_Cosmetics "ColourPop Cosmetics") released a _Sailor Moon-_ inspired makeup collection.[179] Celebrating the 25th anniversary of _Sailor Moon_ in the U.S., streetwear brand KITH released clothing such as hoodies and t-shirts with _Sailor Moon_ graphics on them.[180] In honor of _Sailor Moon'_ s 30th anniversary, brands like [Sanrio](/wiki/Sanrio "Sanrio"), [Uniqlo](/wiki/Uniqlo "Uniqlo"), and Maison de FLEUR launched a _Sailor Moon_ collaboration in January 2022.[181][182][183]
